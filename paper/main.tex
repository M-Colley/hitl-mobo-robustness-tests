\begin{abstract}
Human-in-the-loop Bayesian optimization is often evaluated on oracles fitted to archival ratings, confounding feedback noise with oracle error; we use twenty analytic landscapes and identically seeded clean twins instead. At one landscape SD of rating error from the first rating, the deployed design loses $29.7\%$ of the achievable improvement, $42\%$ $[34, 50]$ of it selection; no search-side change we tested reduced the deployed loss. Oracles fitted to synthetic ratings of the same landscapes do not significantly understate it (trees: $1.02$ $[0.88, 1.19]$), but their reported search loss depends on the model family. At one SD from the first rating, for LogEI and qNEI, shipping by the posterior mean less one posterior SD recovers $10\%$ $[2, 18]$ of the loss on held-out seeds ($4\%$ $[-2, 11]$ on the training seeds, which pick a more cautious rule; price $0.021$ without error), and a final comparative sitting adds nothing measurable; when five-SD error starts at trial 21 of 50, a sitting of sixteen recovers $41\%$ $[30, 50]$ (price $0.033$). Refitting on ranks recovers $51\%$ $[36, 63]$ when $15\%$ of ratings are gross faults (price $0.029$). If three human studies' rater noise transfers, the loss is $22$--$32\%$ ($0$--$42\%$ across intervals).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Bayesian optimization (BO) is the standard tool for optimizing an expensive black-box function from few evaluations \citep{jones1998efficient, shahriari2016taking, frazier2018tutorial}. It is also used for objectives that only a person can evaluate: interface parameters judged for trust or aesthetics, generated content ranked for quality, robot behaviours rated for comfort. This is human-in-the-loop (HITL) optimization \citep{brochu2010tutorial, gonzalez2017preferential, koyama2017sequential, khajah2016designing, dudley2019crowdsourcing, koyama2020sequential, kadner2021adaptifont, chan2022investigating, liao2023interaction}. The optimizer assumes each observation is informative about the objective, but a person is not a sensor. People rate inconsistently, drift, are generous or harsh, make correlated errors, and sometimes apply the wrong setting altogether. How much that costs is asked routinely, but has so far been answered with a design that mixes the rater's error with the error of the oracle it is scored against.

A common approach fits a model to archival human responses and treats it as the objective \citep{siivola2021preferential, astudillo2023qeubo, letham2022lookahead}, then injects synthetic error into its responses and measures the degradation \citep{astudillo2023qeubo}. The fitted oracle is itself wrong. In the three archival studies with open data that we draw on \citep{colley2025ehmi, jansen2025opticarvis, susak2026provoice}, its cross-validated $R^2$ is $0.55$ at best (\texttt{ehmi}), $0.23$ for that study when whole participants are held out, and negative for the other two on the pipeline's own folds (Appendix~\ref{app:archival}). The measured degradation therefore mixes the injected error with the oracle's misspecification, and regret is measured against a random-search estimate of the optimum that the optimizer can legitimately beat. The experiment also runs on a single design space, so it cannot say whether its acquisition ranking holds elsewhere.

In this work, we replace the fitted oracle with analytic objectives. On an analytic landscape with a published optimum, the objective is exact, regret is measured from the true optimum, and the geometry of the landscape can be measured before the optimization is run. Twenty such landscapes turn ``which acquisition is robust here'' into a distribution over landscapes, which we report one landscape at a time. Six of them, human-performance laws fixed as a smooth, low-modal subset before any result was computed, stand in for the objectives HITL studies optimize.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{A protocol with no fitted oracle, and what error costs HITL optimization.} Twenty analytic landscapes with verified optima, four error processes at two onsets, identically seeded clean twins and a model-free control. At $1\sigma$ from the first rating the deployed loss is $29.7\%$ of the achievable improvement, $18$--$47\%$ on every landscape and $22$--$32\%$ at the rater noise of three human studies if their unit transfers (Section~\ref{sec:cost}). Oracles fitted to synthetic ratings of the same landscapes, the common evaluation design, do not significantly understate the deployed loss in any of three model families (tree ensembles report $1.02$ $[0.88, 1.19]$ of it), but the search loss they report on the trajectory depends on the family (tree ensembles $0.72$ $[0.56, 0.93]$, significant under two of four ways of setting the achievable improvement; Gaussian processes and MLPs $1.04$ to $1.41$ under all four). Exact objectives are needed because a fitted oracle is misspecified, because what it reports of the search loss and how it ranks landscapes depend on the model family and on how its achievable improvement is set, and because only an exact objective has a verified optimum and a geometry measurable before any run.
  \item \textbf{Where the cost sits.} Deployed regret splits into search loss and selection loss (Section~\ref{sec:decomp}). Under the standard ship rule, at $1\sigma$ from the first rating selection is $42\%$ $[34, 50]$ of the cost of error, rising to $83\%$ $[75, 90]$ at $5\sigma$ from trial 21, and it is zero under exact observation. Most search-side changes we ran move both terms, and none leads to a gain on the deployed design.
  \item \textbf{What helps, by how much, and what does not.} We test procedures that act on the final decision, re-make every choice among their variants on training seeds and score it on held-out ones, and replay the sitting of up to twelve designs, the ship rules and the confirmation test on ten untouched seeds under \textsc{gaussian} error (Section~\ref{sec:budget}). At $1\sigma$ from the first rating (LogEI and qNEI, four processes), shipping by the posterior mean less one latent SD recovers $10\%$ $[2, 18]$ of the cost on held-out seeds but $4\%$ $[-2, 11]$ on the training seeds, which pick the posterior mean less two latent SDs ($10\%$ $[1, 18]$ held out), at a price of $0.021$ of the achievable improvement without error, and a final sitting of two designs adds nothing measurable to it. At $5\sigma$ from trial 21 a sitting of sixteen recovers $41\%$ $[30, 50]$ on held-out seeds and gains $0.249$ $[0.164, 0.339]$ more than that rule, at a price of $0.033$. Four confirmation trials cut false claims of improvement from $15.7\%$ to $0.5\%$, at the price of detecting $51\%$ of real improvements instead of $88\%$. Refitting on the ratings' ranks, which assumes nothing about the fault, recovers $51\%$ $[36, 63]$ of the cost when $15\%$ of trials carry a gross fault (price $0.029$), more than relevance pursuit, an explicit model of the fault ($38\%$ $[24, 51]$, price $0.011$; \autoref{tab:remedies}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Bayesian optimization under noise.} Noise has been part of the formulation since \citet{jones1998efficient} and is routine in practice \citep{snoek2012practical}; acquisitions built for it include augmented expected improvement \citep{huang2006global}, the knowledge gradient \citep{frazier2009knowledge} and noisy expected improvement \citep{letham2019constrained, balandat2020botorch}. \citet{picheny2013benchmark} benchmark infill criteria under noise on analytic test functions, the closest antecedent; we differ in treating the error \emph{process} and its onset as factors and in asking where the cost sits. Heteroscedastic and non-stationary surrogates \citep{kersting2007most, cowenrivers2022hebo}, outlier-robust likelihoods \citep{martinezcantin2018practical, ament2024robust}, corruption-tolerant acquisitions \citep{bogunovic2020corruption} and time-varying bandits \citep{bogunovic2016time} each model one of our error processes; we test representatives of the first two as remedies (Appendices~\ref{app:budgetneutral} and~\ref{app:adapt}). The regret of the best design visited and of the one recommended, simple and inference regret, are both standard in noisy-BO benchmarking \citep{picheny2013benchmark}; we measure their gap under feedback error. Shipping the best observed rating selects on noise, so the shipped design's rating overstates its value, the optimizer's curse of decision analysis \citep{smith2006optimizer}. Selection loss is the regret the same selection on noise causes. The same bias inflates a claim that the shipped design improves on the start, which inference on winners corrects \citep{andrews2024inference} and Section~\ref{sec:budget} tests on fresh ratings. The ship rule that closes the gap when the surrogate is right, the posterior-mean maximiser, is the one the knowledge gradient optimizes for \citep{frazier2009knowledge}; Section~\ref{sec:decomp} scores it. Spending the end of a budget on choosing among visited designs is best-arm identification \citep{audibert2010best} and ranking-and-selection \citep{kim2006selecting}, the clean-up step after simulation optimization \citep{boesel2003using}; kriging-based optimizers with such a step have been compared on test functions under noise \citep{jalali2017comparison}.

\paragraph{Human-in-the-loop and preference-based optimization.} Where the objective is a person's judgement, the usual response is to change the observation model: pairwise preferences with an ordinal likelihood \citep{chu2005preference, gonzalez2017preferential, astudillo2023qeubo}, or queries a crowd can answer as a line search \citep{koyama2017sequential}. \citet{chan2022investigating} document the benefits and drawbacks from the user's side. Reward models fitted to human labels serve as the evaluation oracle in preference learning at scale \citep{stiennon2020learning, ouyang2022training, gao2023scaling}. The oracle-isolation experiment of Section~\ref{sec:cost} tests the same construction with tree-ensemble, Gaussian-process and MLP oracles on low-dimensional objectives. A comparison is immune by construction to a strictly monotone fault shared across a sitting; this removes the effect of a noise-free drift, though in the comparison arm at a price larger than that effect, and Appendix~\ref{app:elicitation} finds it does not help against a saturating scale.

\paragraph{The landscape as the unit of replication.} Results on analytic test functions need not transfer \citep{eggensperger2021hpobench}. The landscape is therefore our unit of replication, and where the observational design cannot identify an effect, we manipulate the geometry directly.

\section{Setup}

\subsection{Landscapes}
Twenty analytic landscapes with verified optima, $d = 2$ to $11$: thirteen classical test functions, six human-performance laws including the Yerkes--Dodson inverted-U \citep{yerkes1908relation}, and moving peaks \citep{branke1999memory} (Appendix~\ref{app:descriptors}). The six laws are smooth with at most one interior mode, like the preference and performance objectives HITL studies optimize; four are additive across their inputs and four have a corner optimum (Appendix~\ref{app:smooth}). We fixed them as the smooth subset before computing any result. Each objective is affinely standardised to zero mean and unit variance over its box on $2^{16}$ scrambled-Sobol points, so a single grid of error magnitudes serves outputs spanning seven orders of magnitude, with the argmax unchanged. $\optz$, the number of landscape standard deviations (landscape SDs) by which the optimum exceeds the mean, runs from $0.76$ to $56.8$.

\subsection{Error processes}
\label{sec:errors}
Let $g(x)$ be the standardised objective, $t$ the trial, $t_0$ the onset and $\sigma_e$ the swept magnitude, in landscape SDs. Observations are exact for $t \le t_0$. After that, the optimizer sees $\tilde g_t$:
\begin{align}
  \text{\textsc{gaussian}}\quad & \tilde g_t = g(x_t) + \epsilon_t,
    & \epsilon_t &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_e^2) \\
  \text{\textsc{bias}}\quad & \tilde g_t = g(x_t) + \sigma_e + \epsilon_t,
    & \epsilon_t &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_e^2) \\
  \text{\textsc{drift}}\quad & \tilde g_t = g(x_t)
    + \sigma_e\tfrac{t - t_0}{T - t_0} + \epsilon_t,
    & \epsilon_t &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_e^2) \\
  \text{\textsc{ar}(1)}\quad & \tilde g_t = g(x_t) + e_t,
    \quad e_t = \rho e_{t-1} + \eta_t,
    & \eta_t &\sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \sigma_e^2(1 - \rho^2)\right)
\end{align}
with $\rho = 0.8$ and $e_{t_0+1}$ stationary. \textsc{bias} is a generous rater, \textsc{drift} fatigue over a session, \textsc{ar}(1) a rater whose consecutive judgements are correlated. These design choices are checked against three archival studies (Appendix~\ref{app:archival}), which show rater offsets and session drift of about this size (though the drift differs between raters). They show little positive serial correlation once each session's own trend is removed, so that \textsc{ar}(1) is a stress test. They also show heavier tails and more saturation than the main arms have, which the gross-fault and capped-scale arms model. We sweep $\sigma_e \in \{0.05, 0.25, 1, 5\}$, the last a stress level, and $t_0 \in \{0, 20\}$ over $T = 50$ trials with $5$ initial random samples, twelve acquisitions and ten seeds: $76{,}800$ noisy runs, each paired with one of $2{,}400$ clean twins (the seeds of every arm are in Appendix~\ref{app:estimands}). Appendix~\ref{app:inputerrorarm} adds slips, errors in the design the person experiences, such as a slider that overshoots, a noisy-input setting \citep{oliveira2019bayesian, frohlich2020noisy} with the perturbation unknown.

\subsection{Optimizer and acquisitions}
\label{sec:optimizer}
Every arm uses the default single-task Gaussian process (GP) of BoTorch 0.18.2.dev23 (commit eea35f51a) \citep{balandat2020botorch}, except where a remedy arm replaces it (Appendices~\ref{app:budgetneutral}, \ref{app:adapt} and~\ref{app:elicitation}): a radial-basis-function (RBF) kernel with dimension-scaled lengthscale priors, inputs normalised to the unit cube, outputs standardised, fitted by marginal likelihood. Ten model-based acquisitions, five strategies in analytic, log \citep{ament2023unexpected} or Monte-Carlo form: expected improvement (EI, LogEI, qEI), probability of improvement (PI, LogPI, qPI), the upper confidence bound \citep{srinivas2010gaussian} (UCB, qUCB), noisy expected improvement \citep{letham2019constrained} (qNEI) and the posterior-mean maximiser (Greedy). There are also two model-free floors, uniform random and scrambled Sobol. Improvement-based acquisitions (EI, PI and their log and Monte-Carlo forms) take as their incumbent the maximum posterior mean at visited points plus a margin $\xi = 0.01$ in objective units (landscape SDs on the analytic landscapes), the usual choice under noise and not a neutral one (Appendix~\ref{sec:incumbent}). UCB and qUCB use $\beta = 4$, two posterior SDs, and qNEI integrates over the best value at the visited designs instead of fixing an incumbent. Each model-based proposal refines the ten best of $1{,}000$ uniform random points by L-BFGS-B for at most $200$ iterations, and the Monte-Carlo acquisitions use $256$ quasi-Monte-Carlo samples ($64$ in the multi-objective arm of Appendix~\ref{sec:multiobjective}); the knowledge gradient of Appendix~\ref{sec:designchoices} is instead optimised jointly with its eight fantasies, from ten restarts initialised from $512$ raw samples.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
\textbf{The primary estimand is deployed loss}: the regret of the deployed design, the visited design with the best observed rating at the final trial (the earliest of them when several share that rating; Appendix~\ref{app:shiprules}), minus the same in the identically seeded clean twin, divided by the landscape's achievable improvement $\optz$. The tables call it the deployed excess. A \emph{ship rule} is how the deployed design is picked from the visited ones. The best observed rating is the standard rule; Appendix~\ref{app:shiprules} scores four others, among them the posterior-mean maximiser that noise-aware practice recommends; Section~\ref{sec:decomp} reports what they recover. \textbf{Search loss} is the regret of the best design \emph{visited}, $y^\star - \max_{t' \le t} g(x_{t'})$; at the final trial, deployed minus search loss is the selection loss of Section~\ref{sec:decomp}. Its post-onset per-trial average, the \emph{trajectory loss}, has lower variance and drives the acquisition ranking and several appendices, but it is bounded by the clean run's improvement after the onset (Section~\ref{sec:cost}). A final value is compared only with a final value. Paired runs share their candidate-proposal stream, and $y^\star$ is a verified supremum. Since a landscape SD is not a fully comparable unit across landscapes (Appendix~\ref{sec:currency}), every aggregate is formed from per-landscape values and every headline is also shown per landscape. Intervals are 95\% percentile landscape bootstraps; at twenty clusters they cover $92$--$93\%$, and a $t$ interval that covers about $95\%$ is up to two points wider (Appendix~\ref{app:estimands}).

\subsection{Controls}
Appendix~\ref{sec:controls} details three controls: the model-free floors never read an observation, and their excess search loss is identically zero over all $12{,}800$ comparisons; no landscape is ceiling-limited by its clean run; and \textsc{bias} from the first rating matches \textsc{gaussian} on the post-onset trajectory excess to within $0.012$ landscape SDs at every magnitude, less than one standard error of the difference ($0.013$ to $0.038$ across magnitudes and clusterings, since the two arms draw independent noise streams), as a GP that standardises its targets requires.

\section{How much does feedback error cost?}
\label{sec:cost}

\autoref{tab:dose} and \autoref{fig:dose} give the cost at every magnitude and onset. Error whose standard deviation equals the landscape's own, present from the first rating, costs the deployed design $29.7\%$ $[26.6, 32.9]$ of the achievable improvement. The search loss is $17.3\%$, so the deployed loss is $1.7\times$ the search loss, and $2.5\times$ at $5\sigma$, where the deployed loss reaches $71.1\%$. \autoref{fig:perlandscape} shows the same cell landscape by landscape: the deployed loss is between $18\%$ (Rosenbrock) and $47\%$ (Hartmann-6) on every landscape (median $29\%$), and every interval excludes zero. The six human-performance laws lose $32.7\%$ $[29, 37]$ against $27.8\%$ $[24, 32]$ for the thirteen classical functions, so the cost is not a product of multimodality (Appendix~\ref{app:smooth}).

\textbf{The late onset looks cheap on the trajectory for a mechanical reason.} There, $1\sigma$ error from trial 21 costs $2.8\%$ against $14.1\%$ from the first rating, an onset ratio of $5.0\times$. But a noisy run and its clean twin share every design until the error arrives, so the post-onset excess can never exceed the improvement the clean run still makes after that point; in $27\%$ of late-onset runs at $1\sigma$ it sits on that bound throughout (Appendix~\ref{app:onsetbound}). The bound is $5.3\times$ smaller at the late onset, and divided by it, error from trial 21 destroys $43\%$ $[34, 51]$ of the improvement still available and error from the first rating $41\%$ $[36, 45]$, a ratio of $0.95$ $[0.78, 1.20]$. The bound also produces the growth of the onset effect with the budget (Appendix~\ref{sec:designchoices}). The deployed design is not bounded this way: there error from trial 21 still costs $13.6\%$ at $1\sigma$, about half the cost from the first rating.

Over the $6{,}400$ cell means of the model-based sweep, main effects account for $24\%$ (magnitude), $17\%$ (onset), $10\%$ (landscape), $4\%$ (acquisition) and $0.2\%$ (error process) of the variance of the trajectory loss as a fraction of $\optz$, the rest being interaction, and within the $1\sigma$, first-rating cell the landscape accounts for $49\%$ and the acquisition for $22\%$. In landscape SDs, which keep the differences in scale between landscapes that dividing by $\optz$ removes, the landscape leads ($32\%$; magnitude $10\%$, onset $3\%$, acquisition $0.7\%$, error process $0.1\%$; $77\%$ and $2\%$ within that cell). The error process's small share is partly built in, since \textsc{bias} from the first rating is \textsc{gaussian} plus an offset the standardised GP cannot see.

\paragraph{Is $1\sigma$ a lot?} Within-rater noise in three published human studies \citep{colley2025ehmi, jansen2025opticarvis, susak2026provoice}, estimated as a nearest-neighbour nugget \citep{cressie1993statistics} over ratings of close designs by the same participant and expressed in $\sigma_f$, the spread of each study's fitted objective, is $0.74$ $[0.48, 0.97]$, $1.11$ $[0.81, 1.40]$ and $0.60$ $[0.00, 0.90]$ $\sigma_f$, with participant-bootstrap intervals (\autoref{tab:anchor}); $\sigma_f$ plays the part the landscape SD plays in the sweep. Neither the second nor the third study's oracle predicts held-out ratings, so their $\sigma_f$ is fragile (another oracle family puts the second estimate at $0.67$; Appendix~\ref{app:archival}). Participants are noisy from their first rating, so on the rows of \autoref{tab:dose} for error from the first rating these studies would lose $22$--$32\%$ on the deployed design, interpolating log-linearly and taking $\sigma_f$ as the landscape SD, and $17$--$32\%$, $24$--$42\%$ and $0$--$31\%$ with each nugget's participant-bootstrap interval propagated (Appendix~\ref{app:archival}). The nugget can understate the noise, and the within-session residual bounds it at $1.2$--$2.4\sigma_f$, where the loss would reach up to about half.

\paragraph{Does a fitted oracle understate it?} The fitted-oracle design is the common alternative. Its companion arm on the three archival datasets reports two thirds to nine tenths of the trajectory loss measured here from the first rating as a share of the achievable improvement (the ratio's interval includes $1$ at every magnitude but $0.25\sigma$), and a third to a half as a fraction of the gap to a model-free floor, but on different landscapes (Appendix~\ref{sec:fitted}). We therefore hold the landscape fixed. For each landscape we build a synthetic archival dataset ($37$ participants, each rating the first $20$ designs of a clean BO run once with noise at $1\sigma_f$), fit an oracle to it by the pipeline's own model selection, whose seven candidate families are all tree ensembles, and rerun the experiment against that oracle, with the error scaled by the seed-7 oracle's $\sigma_f$ and the achievable improvement formed within each run seed's refitted oracle. On the deployed design at $1\sigma$ the fitted oracle reports $1.02$ $[0.88, 1.19]$ of the exact objective's loss (less on $12$ of $20$ landscapes, Wilcoxon $p = 0.87$), with intervals including $1$ at $0.25\sigma$ and $5\sigma$ too; on the trajectory it reports $0.72$ $[0.56, 0.93]$ of the search loss (less on $15$ of $20$, $p = 0.03$). Three other ways of setting the two arms' achievable improvements give $1.12$ to $1.39$ and $0.76$ to $0.84$ at $1\sigma$, so the understatement of search loss is significant under two of the four. As fractions of the floor gap the same runs report $0.20$ $[0.11, 0.62]$ of the deployed loss, a unit near zero for the exact objective on three landscapes and inflated for a tree oracle (Appendix~\ref{sec:fitted}). A tree ensemble is piecewise constant, so we repeated the experiment with a Gaussian process and a two-layer MLP of similar median fidelity forced in its place: they report $1.11$ $[0.89, 1.42]$ and $1.41$ $[1.15, 1.76]$ of the exact search loss and $1.17$ $[0.98, 1.37]$ and $1.21$ $[1.05, 1.37]$ of the deployed loss at $1\sigma$; both report more of the search loss than the trees under every normaliser, while on the deployed design how they compare with the trees depends on the normaliser, since each family sets its own achievable improvement. No family reports significantly less of the deployed loss than the exact objective at any magnitude under any of the four normalisers, while what a fitted-oracle study reports of the search loss, and how it ranks landscapes, depends on the family and on how the achievable improvement is set (Appendix~\ref{sec:fitted}). A fitted oracle is also misspecified (cross-validated $R^2$ at most $0.55$ on the archival ratings, Section~\ref{sec:intro}).

\section{Where the cost is: searching or selecting?}
\label{sec:decomp}

Write $x^\star$ for the optimum of the standardised objective $g$ (Section~\ref{sec:errors}), $V$ for the designs a run visited and $d$ for the one it deploys. Then
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:decomp}
  \underbrace{g(x^\star) - g(d)}_{\text{deployed regret}}
  \;=\;
  \underbrace{g(x^\star) - \max_{x \in V} g(x)}_{\text{search loss}}
  \;+\;
  \underbrace{\max_{x \in V} g(x) - g(d)}_{\text{selection loss}} .
\end{equation}
\autoref{eq:decomp} is an accounting identity, useful as a pair of counterfactuals. Selection loss is the amount that a ship rule that knew the true value of every visited design would recover without a single new trial, so it bounds the most that any change to how a study chooses can gain. Search loss is what would remain with that perfect choice, so under that rule it bounds what a better search can gain on the deployed design; under the standard rule a change to the search also moves the selection loss, in either direction (\autoref{tab:remedysplit}). The selection term is zero under exact observation (the best-rated visited design is then the best visited design). That is why it never appears in a noiseless experiment, and why a study that reports its best-rated design is safe only there. As a check, the largest selection loss over the $2{,}000$ clean runs of the ten model-based acquisitions is exactly zero.

\textbf{A large part of what error costs is selection, and how large depends on the error.} Against the identically seeded clean twin, the selection share rises with the magnitude and with the lateness of the onset, from $31\%$ $[20, 44]$ at $0.05\sigma$ from the first rating to $83\%$ $[75, 90]$ at $5\sigma$ from trial 21 (\autoref{tab:dose}). At $1\sigma$ from the first rating the deployed loss of $0.297$ splits into $0.173$ of search loss and $0.124$ of selection loss, so selection is $41.8\%$ $[34.1, 50.4]$; per landscape it runs from $4\%$ (Shekel) to $85\%$ (Powell) (\autoref{fig:perlandscape}). Pooling every cell would give $61.8\%$, but that pool is dominated by the $5\sigma$ cells and we do not use it.

\textbf{Search-side changes mostly move both terms, and none nets a gain.} Six search-side changes (augmented EI, Thompson sampling, a lower-confidence-bound incumbent, minimum-distance proposals, a slip-aware acquisition and redistributed rating effort) ran as their own arms, with the human trials fixed, each scored against the standard process on the same cells and acquisitions (Appendix~\ref{app:budgetneutral}). None lowers the deployed loss, and, split by \autoref{eq:decomp}, they fail: Thompson sampling \citep{thompson1933likelihood, russo2018tutorial} raises search loss by $5.7$ and selection loss by $7.7$ points relative to the achievable improvement. Minimum-distance proposals lower search loss by $1.9$ $[0.2, 3.7]$ and raise selection loss by $5.7$, a better search that ships a worse design. A lower-confidence-bound incumbent, the one change that lowers selection loss ($2.2$ $[0.7, 4.0]$), raises search loss by $7.6$. Redistributing rating effort across trials raises selection loss by $4.6$ to $10.4$ points, only one interval excluding zero.

\textbf{Pooled over magnitudes, the posterior-mean ship rule recovers little, except where the surrogate fits.} Under \textsc{gaussian} error at $0.25\sigma$ and above (ten acquisitions, seeds 7--16), the posterior-mean maximiser recovers $+0.7\%$ $[-4, +6]$ of the deployed loss and the mean rating $-1.4\%$, although at $n = 50$ the GP's noise estimate has largely recovered ($0.85$ of the injected SD). On the six laws the maximiser recovers $+11.5\%$ $[6, 16]$ and beats the best observed rating on all six, still leaving about nine tenths of the loss in place; on the thirteen classical functions it recovers $-3.3\%$. On clean runs the GP reads the roughness of the ten rugged functions as noise, and across landscapes its leave-one-out fit predicts the rule's gain at Spearman $0.62$ (Appendix~\ref{app:smooth}). The posterior is misspecified where the landscape is rugged, and misled by structured error, losing even on the laws under large late \textsc{drift} ($-15.9\%$; Appendix~\ref{app:smooth}). Rules that discount its uncertainty recover more, at no extra trial but not for free: the posterior mean less one latent SD recovers $7.7\%$ $[2.4, 13.1]$ and less two, the cautious rule of Section~\ref{sec:budget}, $10.1\%$ $[4.6, 15.9]$, each at a price of $0.015$ without error, with two thirds of the cost they are scored against in the $5\sigma$ cells.

\section{Buying identification with the trials you have}
\label{sec:budget}

If much of the cost is selection, two levers remain: a better ship rule, which costs no trial, and human trials spent on identifying rather than searching. The final sitting below is scored against the standard process and, on the same runs, against two ship rules that spend no trial. Every end-of-study procedure changes only the end of a run and is replayed exactly from an existing log (Appendix~\ref{app:endofstudy}); the fault remedies of the last paragraph that change the surrogate or the scale during the run ran as their own arms, and the refit on the ratings' ranks is a replay. Each choice among variants is made on seeds 7--11 and scored on seeds 12--16, with landscape splits as a second check (Appendix~\ref{app:heldout}, \autoref{tab:heldout}).

\textbf{A final comparison.} We hold $T = 50$ and spend the last $k$ trials on one sitting: the top $k$ visited designs by the posterior mean less one latent SD are each shown once, and the best-rated is deployed. The sitting is modelled as simultaneous, so error shared across it, including the \textsc{drift} ramp and the \textsc{ar}(1) state, cancels, and the looks carry the full idiosyncratic noise (Appendix~\ref{app:endofstudy} lets the ramp and the state move through the sitting; the sizes chosen at $1\sigma$ from the first rating and at $5\sigma$ from trial 21 stay the same). The useful size depends on the magnitude and the onset (\autoref{fig:kcurve}, \autoref{tab:sittingcell}). At $1\sigma$ from the first rating, the regime of the anchor in Section~\ref{sec:cost}, seeds 7--11 and seeds 12--16 both choose $k = 2$. For LogEI and qNEI, pooled over the four processes, it gains $+0.030$ $[+0.012, +0.050]$ of the achievable improvement on the deployed design at the final trial on seeds 12--16, $12\%$ $[5, 18]$ of the cost of error, on $14$ of $20$ landscapes and with a positive mean under each process; it survives Holm's correction over the nine values of $k$ ($p = 0.03$), and random landscape splits choose it in $88\%$ of folds. On the replication arm's ten seeds (27--36), not used to choose or score any procedure, it gains $+0.028$ $[+0.006, +0.049]$ under \textsc{gaussian} error, $11\%$ of the cost, on $14$ of $20$ landscapes (Wilcoxon $p = 0.02$). Larger sittings lose in this cell ($-0.010$ $[-0.026, +0.006]$ for $k = 12$ and $-0.025$ $[-0.045, -0.006]$ for $k = 16$ on seeds 7--16). Pooled over magnitudes and onsets the curve is flat at $+0.020$ to $+0.022$ from $k = 5$ to $16$, carried by the $5\sigma$ cell from trial 21: without it the pooled gain of $k = 12$ is $0.000$ $[-0.012, +0.012]$ (Appendix~\ref{app:heldout}).

\textbf{At moderate noise the gain belongs to the ship rule the sitting builds on.} Shipping the design the sitting would rank first, by the posterior mean less one latent SD after all $50$ trials, gains $+0.027$ $[+0.006, +0.048]$ at $1\sigma$ from the first rating on the same runs on seeds 12--16, $10\%$ $[2, 18]$ of the cost, but $+0.011$ $[-0.005, +0.025]$ on seeds 7--11, $4\%$ $[-2, 11]$, where the cautious rule below gains more and would be chosen ($10\%$ $[1, 18]$ on seeds 12--16, price $0.023$). The sitting's paired increment over the first rule is $+0.003$ $[-0.003, +0.009]$ on seeds 12--16 (Wilcoxon $p = 0.39$) and $+0.010$ $[0.000, +0.022]$ on seeds 7--11, and over the cautious rule $+0.004$ $[-0.002, +0.011]$ on seeds 12--16; under \textsc{gaussian} error the two increments are $+0.008$ and $+0.013$ on seeds 12--16 and $-0.008$ and $-0.002$ on the untouched seeds, where the first rule gains $14\%$ $[5, 21]$ of the cost (Appendix~\ref{app:heldout}). Without error the sitting costs $0.018$ of the achievable improvement on seeds 12--16 and the rule $0.021$; pooled over magnitudes and onsets the rule gains $+0.001$ and the cautious rule $+0.008$ (seeds 7--16; Appendix~\ref{app:heldout}).

\textbf{Where a sitting beats the ship rules that spend no trial.} Large structured error arriving late misleads the posterior. At $5\sigma$ from trial 21 (four processes, seeds 12--16) the first rule gains $-0.022$ $[-0.045, -0.002]$ of the achievable improvement on the deployed design and the cautious rule, chosen on seeds 7--11 here, $+0.009$ $[-0.017, +0.037]$. A sitting of sixteen gains $+0.227$ $[+0.147, +0.321]$, $41\%$ $[30, 50]$ of the cost, at a price of $0.033$, and is $+0.249$ $[+0.164, +0.339]$ and $+0.218$ $[+0.146, +0.291]$ ahead of the two rules on $18$ and $17$ of $20$ landscapes (Holm-adjusted $p < 0.001$ over the eight cells' chosen sizes), mostly under \textsc{drift}, \textsc{ar}(1) and \textsc{bias}, and whether or not the ramp and the \textsc{ar}(1) state move through the sitting. Under \textsc{gaussian} error alone both rules do well ($+0.113$ and $+0.145$) and the sitting's lead is $+0.097$ $[+0.030, +0.162]$ and $+0.065$ $[+0.010, +0.122]$ ($p = 0.12$ and $0.29$ after Holm over the nine $k$); on the untouched seeds a sitting of twelve, the largest replayed there, leads by $+0.111$ $[+0.048, +0.174]$ and $+0.054$ $[0.000, +0.113]$, against $+0.091$ and $+0.059$ for twelve on seeds 12--16 (Appendix~\ref{app:heldout}).

\textbf{A model-based choice of $k$ does worse than a fixed one.} Choosing $k$ per run from the surrogate's posterior and the run's rate of improvement gains $-0.007$ $[-0.022, +0.006]$ of the achievable improvement on the deployed design on the held-out seeds, $0.024$ $[0.009, 0.039]$ less than the $k = 12$ chosen on seeds 7--11, at a price of $0.022$ $[0.007, 0.045]$ without error ($0.024$ for $k = 12$). It decides once, at trial 20, so under error from trial 21 it decides on exact ratings, and all of its shortfall is there; a form built after this result, which decides at each $T - k$, does not beat a fixed $k$ either (Appendices~\ref{app:budgetrule} and~\ref{app:heldout}).

\textbf{A cautious ship rule, at no extra trial.} Shipping the visited design with the highest posterior mean less two latent SDs, a kriging-quantile rule \citep{picheny2013quantile}, recovers $10.1\%$ $[4.6, 15.9]$ of the deployed loss on seeds 7--16 under \textsc{gaussian} error at $0.25\sigma$ and above, $10.7\%$ $[5, 16]$ on the held-out seeds, $9.6\%$ $[5.3, 13.4]$ on the untouched seeds and $13.9\%$ $[11, 17]$ on the six laws (Appendix~\ref{app:smooth}), but two thirds of the cost behind the $10.1\%$ sits in the $5\sigma$ cells. At $1\sigma$ it recovers $7.7\%$ $[2.7, 12.8]$ over both onsets ($5.8\%$ $[2, 10]$ from the first rating), and over $0.25\sigma$ and $1\sigma$ alone $4.6\%$ $[-2.9, 11.2]$. It pays $0.015$ of the achievable improvement whether or not error is present and loses at $0.25\sigma$ ($-5.1\%$ $[-23, +8]$), so it breaks even somewhere between $0.25\sigma$ and $1\sigma$ and should not be used without an estimate of the rater's noise.

\textbf{Confirm before claiming.} A study also claims that its design beats where it started. The standard process claims an improvement in $98\%$ of runs, and $15.7\%$ of runs make a false claim, $42.7\%$ at $5\sigma$ from the first rating (\textsc{gaussian} error, LogEI, qNEI and UCB, seeds 7--16). Spending four final trials rating the chosen and the initial design twice each, and claiming only if a one-sided paired $t$-test at $\alpha = 0.05$ prefers the chosen one, takes the false-claim rate to $0.5\%$ ($15.5\%$ to $0.6\%$ on seeds 27--36). The test's price is power: it detects $51\%$ of the real improvements, where claiming on the fresh mean alone detects $88\%$, and $26\%$ at $1\sigma$ from the first rating (Appendix~\ref{app:endofstudy}).

\textbf{Pooled over magnitudes, neither the sitting nor the cautious rule survives a family-wise correction.} On the held-out seeds, with a Wilcoxon test across landscapes and Holm's correction \citep{holm1979simple} over the $27$ procedures of the core family, the pooled sitting over the top five (\textsc{gaussian} error, LogEI, qNEI and UCB, all magnitudes and onsets; gaining on $15$ of $20$ landscapes) and the cautious rule (\textsc{gaussian} error at $0.25\sigma$ and above, ten acquisitions; $16$ of $20$) do not survive (adjusted $p = 0.09$ and $0.06$), and with Holm over all $41$ procedures no fixed $k$ gains (Appendix~\ref{app:heldout}).

\textbf{Remedies for gross faults, rater offsets and saturation.} \autoref{tab:remedies} collects every remedy that helped, by fault, with its scope and price (Appendices~\ref{app:budgetneutral}--\ref{app:detector}). The largest recovery for gross faults comes from a remedy that assumes nothing about the fault: refitting the surrogate on the ratings' ranks recovers $51\%$ $[36, 63]$ of the cost when $15\%$ of trials carry a fault of $20$ SDs at $0.25\sigma$ from the first rating ($49\%$ $[34, 63]$ held out), at a price of $0.029$ on those runs, and under the main sweep's error, which has no gross faults, it recovers $-1\%$ $[-11, +8]$, so it deserves the same caution as the cautious rule. Explicit models of the fault recover less (relevance pursuit $38\%$ $[24, 51]$ at a price of $0.011$, and $-30\%$ $[-119, +12]$ when only $5\%$ of trials carry a fault) or cannot be deployed as run (clipping to the landscape's known range, $22$--$44\%$ by tie rule, whose range ends at the known optimum and whose zero price holds by construction). Against a saturating scale the rank refit recovers $36\%$ (price $0.029$) and re-anchoring the cap $25\%$ (zero price by construction) as logged, and about a fifth with tied ratings at the cap broken at random ($23\%$ $[17, 28]$ and $18\%$ $[9, 25]$); re-anchoring helps only at $0.25\sigma$, and at $1\sigma$ it recovers $-7\%$ $[-15, +1]$, or $-17\%$ $[-25, -10]$ with random ties. Every change to the search failed, as did a heavy-tailed likelihood, replication, re-rating the incumbent, the knowledge gradient and giving the GP the true noise variance (\autoref{tab:adapt}, \autoref{tab:budgetneutral}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

If the rater noise measured in three human studies transfers to the landscape scale, feedback error costs the deployed design $22$--$32\%$ of the improvement it could have had, where a clean run's shipped design falls $13\%$ of the achievable improvement short of the optimum. For evaluations that treat an oracle fitted to human ratings as the ground truth, the isolation experiment gives no evidence that they understate the deployed design's cost of error, but what they report of the search loss, and how they rank landscapes, depends on the model family and on how the achievable improvement is set. Four consequences follow. Report the deployed design, since the trajectory ranks acquisitions differently and inflates the onset effect (the rankings correlate at $0.59$; the onset ratio is $5\times$ against $2.2\times$). Estimate the rater's noise relative to the objective's spread before the study: the magnitude decides more than the kind of error does, and with the onset it decides which end-of-study procedure pays; a one-shot predictor computed from the objective adds to the magnitude in landscape SDs without replacing it, and a clean ten-trial pilot approximates it (Appendices~\ref{app:mediation} and~\ref{app:pilot}). Spend the end of the budget on the decision. A confirmation test before any claim of improvement cuts false claims thirtyfold, at the price of four trials and of detecting $51\%$ of real improvements instead of $88\%$. At moderate noise, ship by the posterior mean less one or two latent SDs, which costs no trial and, for LogEI and qNEI at $1\sigma$ from the first rating, recovers about a tenth of the loss on held-out seeds, less on the seeds that chose the rule, at about $0.02$ of the achievable improvement without error (Section~\ref{sec:budget}); a final sitting of two designs adds nothing measurable to it. Both rules pay their price whether or not error is present, and the cautious one breaks even somewhere between $0.25\sigma$ and $1\sigma$ and loses below that, so neither should be used without an estimate of the rater's noise. A larger sitting beats both rules only when large structured error arrives mid-study (under \textsc{gaussian} error alone its lead over the cautious rule is marginal), and there a change detector that freezes the study recovers $42$--$64\%$ of the cost on held-out seeds, at a price of $0.028$. Where gross faults are likely, refit on the ratings' ranks, which recovers half their cost with no model of the fault but pays $0.027$ without error and recovers nothing under ordinary rating noise; explicit models of a fault recover less, clipping to the known range needs the known optimum, and the remedies for a saturating scale recover about a fifth once tied ratings are broken at random, re-anchoring the cap helping only at $0.25\sigma$ (\autoref{tab:remedies}). Two mechanisms remain open: why a taller optimum, once broad enough to be found, costs more than a scale-free error allows (Appendices~\ref{sec:currency} and~\ref{sec:manipulation}), and the incumbent's opposite effects on the deployed design and the trajectory (Appendix~\ref{sec:incumbent}).

\paragraph{Limitations.} The landscapes are analytic and the raters simulated. The loss appears on every landscape and, from the first rating, is as large on the six laws as on the classical functions (Section~\ref{sec:cost}). Its transfer to people rests on the noise ratio the archival studies measure, about $0.6$ to $1.1\sigma_f$, inside the sweep, with the serial structure less well identified; the anchor passes through a fitted objective's $\sigma_f$, and the cost at a given magnitude also depends on a landscape's $\optz$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:currency}), which the anchor does not carry. An anchor from repeated ratings is future work. The four main processes are additive and unbounded. A scale capped at the landscape's $0.9$ quantile costs the deployed design $185\%$ $[132, 258]$ more than the same rating noise on an unbounded scale, or $135\%$ $[92, 196]$ more if tied ratings at the cap are broken at random rather than in favour of the earliest, as the standard rule breaks them (Appendix~\ref{app:budgetneutral}), so the headline may understate the loss on scales that saturate, and on capped, clipped or rounded scales the tie-break moves a remedy's recovery by up to $22$ points (Appendix~\ref{app:shiprules}). The oracle-isolation arm covers three oracle families fitted to synthetic ratings, with error grids scaled to the seed-7 oracle (the other seeds' GP and MLP oracles have $0.57$ to $1.98$ times its $\sigma_f$, the trees' $0.94$ to $1.08$), a random-search estimate of the optimum that the GP and MLP runs beat on $59$ and $61$ of $100$ landscape-seeds, and family comparisons that depend on the normaliser, on the deployed design even in sign (Appendix~\ref{sec:fitted}). The per-magnitude analysis of the sitting followed the pooled one; in the reported cell its choice of $k$ agrees in both seed directions and its gain over the standard process replicates on ten untouched seeds ($p = 0.02$), but it adds nothing measurable to the zero-trial rules on either set of seeds ($+0.003$ and $+0.004$ held out, $-0.008$ and $-0.002$ under \textsc{gaussian} error on the untouched seeds), and over all eight cells Holm's adjusted $p$ is $0.23$. The sitting is modelled as simultaneous; letting the drift ramp and the \textsc{ar}(1) state move through it moves no reported pooled gain of the fixed-allocation sitting by more than $0.015$, but order effects beyond the simulated processes, such as contrast or anchoring between designs, are untested, and the best-arm sitting was replayed under the simultaneous model only. In the slip arm the looks carry an assumed rating-noise SD of $0.25$ landscape SDs, and the sitting's recovery of $23.8\%$ $[16, 31]$ (price $0.024$) ranges from $31.9\%$ to $17.8\%$ over SDs of $0$ to $0.5$ (prices $0.012$ to $0.030$). Of the observation-model remedies, the rank rule is scored on held-out seeds and survives Holm's correction in the scope of its recoveries with the earliest tied design shipped (no test was run with random ties at the cap); clipping, relevance pursuit and per-rater offsets ran on five seeds; the halo backfit ran on four multi-objective problems, which allow no test; and the fitted-oracle replication row rests on three datasets. The multi-objective arm cannot separate the hypervolume acquisitions, and the replication planned in advance is void on its own control rule; its seeds serve instead as the untouched test set (Appendix~\ref{app:heldout}).

\section{Design, controls and estimands}
\label{app:partA}

\subsection{The three controls}
\label{sec:controls}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Model-free floor.} \texttt{Random} and \texttt{Sobol} never read an observation, so their excess search loss must be identically zero, and across all $12{,}800$ such comparisons the largest absolute value is exactly zero. The analysis raises rather than warns if it is not, and the table reads the value from that check rather than printing a constant. This negative control shows that no observation noise reaches the candidate stream. The floors are then excluded from the robustness ranking, which a method that ignores data would otherwise win.
  \item \textbf{Headroom.} Where the clean run already reaches the optimum a null is guaranteed, not earned. The smallest clean-run advantage over the floor is $0.176$ (Rosenbrock), so no landscape is ceiling-limited.
  \item \textbf{Uniform offset.} A constant added to every observation is invisible to a GP that standardises its targets and uses a posterior-mean incumbent. With a shared noise stream the two arms propose identical designs to below $10^{-9}$ for the initial design and three model-based candidates (asserted by test), after which floating-point differences are amplified. The sweep draws the two arms' noise independently, so there they can differ by sampling error. From the first rating their post-onset trajectory excess in landscape SDs differs by less than $0.012$ at every magnitude, which is consistent with sampling error: the largest difference, $+0.011$ at $0.25\sigma$, has a standard error of $0.023$ clustered on landscapes and $0.033$ clustered on the noise streams the landscapes share.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Estimands, intervals and seeds}
\label{app:estimands}

One estimand is primary: the deployed design's excess regret at the final trial over its identically seeded clean twin, as a fraction of the landscape's achievable improvement $\optz$, averaged within a landscape over acquisitions and seeds and then over landscapes. Every other number is one of six derived quantities, named where it is used: (i) the final-trial search loss and (ii) the selection loss, the two terms of \autoref{eq:decomp}, which sum to the primary estimand; (iii) the trajectory loss, the post-onset per-trial average of the search-loss excess, used for the acquisition ranking, the predictor and the currency test because it has lower variance (as a fraction of $\optz$ for the ranking, \autoref{fig:frag} and the pilot, and in landscape SDs for the regressions of \autoref{tab:descriptors} and \autoref{tab:mediation}, the manipulated landscapes of \autoref{tab:manipulation} and the currency test, since those models contain $\optz$ or are compared with models that do), and bounded as Appendix~\ref{app:onsetbound} shows; (iv) the share of the standard process's cost of error that a process change recovers, $(R^{\text{std}}_{\text{noisy}} - R^{\text{adapt}}_{\text{noisy}})/(R^{\text{std}}_{\text{noisy}} - R^{\text{std}}_{\text{clean}})$, a ratio of landscape means (Appendix~\ref{app:adapt}); and (v) the gain over the standard process in units of the achievable improvement, the numerator of (iv), used where the cost in the denominator is near zero or pooled over magnitudes that differ by two decades, as for the $k$-curve; and (vi) the price of a process change, its clean run's regret less the standard process's clean run's in the same units, given beside every recovery. Where a normaliser other than $\optz$ is needed, because a Pareto problem has none or a fitted oracle's optimum is estimated (\autoref{tab:fitted}, \autoref{tab:mo}, \autoref{tab:oracleiso}), the same excess is also given as a fraction of the floor gap, the gap between the optimum and a same-budget model-free floor.

\textbf{Intervals.} Intervals are percentile intervals from $2000$ landscape resamples. With twenty clusters such an interval is known to run narrow, so we checked it by simulation, treating the empirical distribution of the twenty landscapes as the population and drawing $10{,}000$ studies of twenty landscapes from it. For the selection share at $1\sigma$, the shortlist recovery and the deployed loss at $1\sigma$ the percentile interval covers $92.2\%$, $92.9\%$ and $92.7\%$ of the time, BCa $92.1$--$94.0\%$, and a $t$ interval with 19 degrees of freedom and a delta-method standard error $94.6$--$94.7\%$, at up to two points more width; for the selection share the $t$ interval is $[32.7, 51.0]$, against $[33.6, 50.2]$ for the percentile interval from $20{,}000$ resamples (Section~\ref{sec:decomp} prints the $2000$-resample percentile interval, $[34.1, 50.4]$). The shortfall is width, not skew, which BCa cannot fix. The check cannot detect bias the empirical distribution shares, or dependence between landscapes, which share seeds by design. The currency test (Appendix~\ref{sec:currency}) is the only place the paper uses BCa intervals \citep{efron1987better}.

\textbf{Seeds.} \autoref{tab:arms} lists the seeds, landscapes and acquisitions of every arm. Procedures that change only the end of a run are replayed from the logs of the arm they modify and add no runs; wherever a choice among such procedures is reported, it is made on seeds 7--11 and scored on seeds 12--16 (Appendix~\ref{app:heldout}).

\subsection{Does noise ever help?}
\label{app:beneficial}

A noisy observation can in principle help, by pushing an over-exploiting optimizer out of a local basin. At one seed per cell, $25$ of the $640$ landscape-by-condition cells had negative mean excess regret with $d_z < -0.2$, noise apparently improving the outcome, which is the kind of finding that invites a mechanism.

At ten seeds it is one cell: \texttt{michalewicz} under \textsc{ar}(1) error at $0.05\sigma$ from trial 21, mean excess $-0.136$, $d_z = -0.21$ over $100$ paired runs. One cell in $640$ is fewer than the fifteen or so that a null family of $640$ tests would flag at this threshold. We report it as a null and note the sampling-variability lesson: the twenty-four cells that disappeared between one seed and ten had all shown noise helping.

\section{The cost of error}
\label{app:partB}

\subsection{The conclusions under seven further checks}
\label{sec:robustness}

\paragraph{The surrogate's noise estimate is not the problem.} The GP's fitted noise is an eighth of the injected value over the first fifteen observations at $1\sigma$ and above, yet giving it the true variance removes about $3\%$ of the summed post-onset trajectory loss over the eight cells, each arm measured against its own clean run, $-1\%$ at $1\sigma$ from the first rating, and survives correction in no condition (Appendix~\ref{sec:noisediag}). Measured against the standard process it recovers $-3\%$ of the trajectory loss and $-3\%$ of the deployed design's, at a price without error of $0.4$ points of the achievable improvement on the trajectory and none on the deployed design, so neither estimand finds a gain (Appendix~\ref{app:adapt}).

\paragraph{The incumbent is part of the method.} The observed-max incumbent removes $20.0\%$ of the summed post-onset trajectory loss, each arm measured against its own clean run, $24\%$ at $1\sigma$ from the first rating and $48\%$ and $64\%$ at $0.25\sigma$ from the first rating and from trial 21 (at $0.05\sigma$ the reference loss is below $0.01$ of the achievable improvement and negative on some landscapes, so only the change is given, $-0.007$ from the first rating and $-0.002$ from trial 21), and, against the standard process, recovers $-10\%$ $[-15, -5]$ of the cost of error on the deployed design at a price of $0.2$ points without error: robustness rankings rank acquisition-and-incumbent pairs (Appendices~\ref{sec:incumbent} and~\ref{app:adapt}).

\paragraph{$\optz$ is not causal on its own, and dimension is not inert.} Purpose-built landscapes show that raising a narrow optimum never measurably raises the cost ($-0.001$ $[-0.035, +0.036]$ at $1\sigma$), that a Levy ladder confounds dimension with signal strength, and that with $\optz$ fixed dimension still raises the cost at $1\sigma$ and $5\sigma$, $1.7\times$ $[1.1, 5.3]$ and $1.7\times$ $[1.4, 2.3]$ from $d = 7$ to $11$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:manipulation}).

\paragraph{Multi-objective BO is not clearly more or less fragile.} On six BoTorch problems the dose-response and onset structures of the trajectory loss replicate and the four hypervolume acquisitions are indistinguishable. On the trajectory and as a fraction of the floor gap, error from the first rating costs a seventh of the scalar suite's at $0.05\sigma$, a third at $0.25\sigma$ and a half at $1\sigma$ and $5\sigma$, the intervals separating only at the two smallest. Measured against what its clean run still gains after the prefix it shares with the noisy run, the running-maximum bound of Appendix~\ref{app:onsetbound}, it loses a comparable share ($46\%$ $[36, 61]$ against $36\%$ $[33, 43]$ of that gain at $1\sigma$, post-onset search loss), and its flatter onset ratio largely reflects that bound. The front such a study would report is another matter: at $1\sigma$ from the first rating about half of it is truly dominated, and in all eight cells its hypervolume shortfall exceeds that of the evaluated set (Appendix~\ref{sec:multiobjective}).

\paragraph{The budget matters, through the onset bound. The rating scale cuts both ways.} With the late onset held at $40\%$ of the run, the onset ratio is $2.3\times$, $5.1\times$ and $11.7\times$ at $T = 25$, $50$ and $100$, and about $1\times$ at each once divided by the clean run's post-onset improvement; a discretised rating scale makes small errors costlier and large ones marginally cheaper (Appendices~\ref{sec:designchoices} and~\ref{app:onsetbound}).

\paragraph{The replication is void; post hoc, the claims hold.} Four claims planned in advance ran on ten fresh seeds; one control failed, so the run is void by its own rule, and with that landscape excluded post hoc all four clear their thresholds (Appendix~\ref{sec:confirmatory}).

\paragraph{On the same landscape, no fitted oracle significantly understates the deployed loss, and the oracle family changes the reported search loss.} On three archival datasets the fitted-oracle design reports two thirds to nine tenths of the post-onset search loss the analytic arm measures from the first rating as a share of the achievable improvement, and a third to a half as a fraction of the floor gap, but those are different landscapes. Holding the landscape fixed and fitting the oracle to synthetic archival data drawn from it, the fitted-oracle design reports $0.72$ $[0.56, 0.93]$ of the exact search loss on the post-onset trajectory at $1\sigma$, less on $15$ of $20$ landscapes, and $1.02$ $[0.88, 1.19]$ of the deployed loss at the final trial, less on $12$, as shares of each oracle's achievable improvement; three other ways of setting the two arms' achievable improvements give $0.76$ to $0.84$ and $1.12$ to $1.39$, so the search-loss understatement is significant under two of the four. As fractions of the floor gap it reports $0.27$ and $0.20$, less on $18$ of $20$, a difference that comes from the normaliser. Gaussian-process and MLP oracles of similar median fidelity report $1.11$ and $1.41$ of the trajectory search loss and $1.17$ and $1.21$ of the final deployed loss; at $1\sigma$ the MLP reports more of the search loss than the tree ensembles under every normaliser, and on the deployed design at $1\sigma$ and $5\sigma$ the direction of the family difference depends on the normaliser (Appendix~\ref{sec:fitted}).

\subsection{What makes an error big?}
\label{sec:currency}

Standardising every objective assumes that a landscape's own standard deviation is the right unit for its error. The rival is the improvement available, since a half-SD error is one thing when the optimum stands $0.76$ SDs above a random design and another at $56.8$. We fit the power law
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[R] \;=\; A\,\sigma_e^{\beta_c}\,\optz^{\beta_z}
\end{equation}
to the landscape-by-magnitude cell means of the raw excess regret $R$, using quasi-Poisson estimating equations, the Poisson pseudo-maximum-likelihood estimator of a multiplicative mean \citep{gourieroux1984pseudo, santossilva2006log}, which needs only the mean to be right and keeps the cells where noise helped (Appendix~\ref{app:beneficial}). If the spread is the right unit (\textsc{spread}), $\beta_z = 0$; if the error relative to the achievable gain is, $R = \optz\, h(\sigma_e/\optz)$, and for a power-law $h$ that means $\beta_c + \beta_z = 1$ (\textsc{gain}). Intervals are BCa intervals over resampled landscapes, the unit of replication for a landscape-level covariate.

The cost also grows with $\optz$ faster than \textsc{gain} allows: $\beta_c + \beta_z - 1$ is $+0.36$ to $+0.51$ from the first rating and $+0.76$ to $+0.85$ from trial 21, with intervals excluding zero in six of eight conditions, \textsc{drift} touching it at both onsets; ordinary least squares on the logarithm of the positive cells rejects \textsc{gain} in all eight. \subsection{The onset bound}
\label{app:onsetbound}

Let $s$ be the last design a noisy run shares with its clean twin, $s = \max(t_0 + 1, 5)$: the design at trial $t_0 + 1$ is chosen from exact data, and the first five trials are a uniform initial design. Write $r_n(t)$ and $r_c(t)$ for the two runs' search loss. A running maximum never gets worse, so for $t \ge s$
\begin{equation*}
  r_n(t) - r_c(t) \;\le\; r_n(s) - r_c(t) \;=\; r_c(s) - r_c(t) \;=\; B(t),
\end{equation*}
the improvement the clean run makes after $s$. The bound is exact given the shared prefix, and the prefix is shared in all $73{,}600$ model-based pairs of the main sweep and both budget arms: designs, true values and search loss are identical through $s$ and observations through $t_0$, and no pair exceeds its bound. It binds often after a late onset. At $1\sigma$, $27\%$ of late-onset runs make no progress after trial 21 while their clean twin does ($7\%$ after trial 5 at the early onset), and $43\%$ of post-onset trials after a late onset with $B > 0$ sit exactly on it.

\subsection{The error processes in the archival data}

The three archival studies contain enough repeated structure to check them: several ratings per participant in one session, designs that recur, and in \texttt{provoice} a counterbalanced crossover in which the same design is rated in both conditions of one sitting. A rating is modelled as the design's value plus the rater's error. \textbf{Two defects in the fitted-oracle pipeline's composites.} In \texttt{opticarvis}, $40$ of the $586$ deduplicated ratings were logged on the raw instrument scales ($1$--$5$, $1$--$7$ and $-3$ to $3$) and the rest on the $[-1, 1]$ scale the optimizer saw. Averaged together, they make one design a spike about four times the height of the rest of the surface, which sets that oracle's optimum and its held-out $R^2$ and puts its noise at $3.35\sigma_f$ (the as-logged row of \autoref{tab:anchor}); on the scale-consistent ratings the noise is $0.67$ $[0.49, 0.85]$ with the oracle family selected on the as-logged data and $1.11$ with the one selected on the corrected data, and no model family reaches a positive held-out $R^2$. In \texttt{provoice} one lower-is-better construct entered the composite with the wrong sign. The corrected data drop the off-scale rows and enter the construct with a minus sign, and the fitted-oracle companion arm runs on the corrected data. Both defects touch only the fitted-oracle companion arm of Appendix~\ref{sec:fitted} and the noise anchor; the analytic arm and the oracle-isolation experiment never read these data.

\textbf{What the data show.} The design model predicts held-out participants poorly (leave-one-participant-out $R^2$ $0.23$, $-0.21$ and $0.08$; the pipeline's $0.55$ for \texttt{ehmi} comes from folds that let a participant's own ratings into training), and search progress rises with trial order, so drift and serial dependence cannot be fully separated from progress the model misses; the one estimate free of that confound is \texttt{provoice}'s crossover, which gives the drift entry of its column. With that caveat, the four main processes fare differently. \textsc{gaussian} noise of $0.6$ to $1.1\sigma_f$ is what the nugget measures in the pipeline's $\sigma_f$ (\autoref{tab:anchor}), though the nugget can understate the noise, and the within-session residual SD bounds it from above at $1.17$, $1.34$ and $2.37\sigma_f$ ($0.81$ for \texttt{opticarvis} in the units of \autoref{tab:archival}). It can also overstate it, since close designs are not replicates: halving the closeness radius lowers the \texttt{ehmi} nugget by $11\%$ and doubling it raises it by $39\%$. The headline's interpolation inherits each nugget's participant-bootstrap interval: joined with the landscape bootstrap, the deployed loss at the measured noise is $25\%$ $[17, 32]$ for \texttt{ehmi}, $32\%$ $[24, 42]$ for \texttt{opticarvis} and $22\%$ $[0, 31]$ for \texttt{provoice}, whose nugget rests on $16$ close pairs (\texttt{scripts/review\_checks/anchor\_propagation.py}). \textsc{bias} matches in size: raters differ by offsets of $0.7$ to $1.4$ noise SDs, though whether an offset shared by every rater exists cannot be identified from ratings alone. \textsc{drift} matches in size but not in form: sessions drift by $0.4$ to $1.2\sigma_f$, but in both directions and by amounts that differ between raters, rather than on one common ramp. \textsc{ar}(1) with $\rho = 0.8$ does not match: the raw lag-one autocorrelation lies below what the same estimator reads under it in every study, and once each session's own trend is removed it is near zero in two studies and negative in \texttt{opticarvis}, so most of the apparent serial dependence is rater-specific drift. An $\rho = 0.8$ component carrying a minority of the residual variance is not excluded (a third of it in \texttt{ehmi}, with an interval reaching $0.62$), and the \textsc{ar}(1) arm is best read as a stress test. The data also show two faults the main arms omit: tails heavier than Gaussian ($1.7$ to $2.4\%$ of residuals beyond three SDs, against $0.3\%$), which the gross-fault arms model, and saturation, with at least one construct at its best end in $55$ to $83\%$ of ratings and noise that shrinks for the best designs as a bounded scale compresses them. The main arms have no bound; the capped-scale arm of Appendix~\ref{app:budgetneutral}, among the most expensive faults measured, is the realistic one.

To measure this rather than argue it, we run the data-driven simulator on the three archival datasets at the same error magnitudes, expressed in each dataset's own $\sigma_f$ so that ``$1\sigma$'' means the same thing in both arms.

The fitted-oracle arm has no verified optimum, and every run seed refits the oracle, so each dataset has ten oracles. Both denominators of \autoref{tab:fitted} are formed within each seed's oracle from the simulator's estimate of its optimum (the best of $200{,}000$ random points and the training designs), and a dataset's value is the ratio of its seed means; with each seed's best clean value in its place, which a run reaches by construction, the fitted floor-gap fraction at $1\sigma$ from the first rating is $49\%$ rather than $43\%$. \textbf{Across different landscapes, the fitted-oracle design reports a third to a half of the search loss as a fraction of the floor gap, only from the first rating, and two thirds to nine tenths of it as a share of the achievable improvement.} At $1\sigma$ from the first rating the analytic arm puts the loss at $99\%$ of the floor gap (cluster-bootstrap interval $[55, 155]$) and the fitted-oracle arm at $43\%$ (its three datasets range from $24\%$ to $75\%$), a ratio of $2.3$, interval $[1.02, 5.1]$ from resampling the twenty landscapes and the three datasets independently; three datasets allow only ten distinct resamples. At $0.05\sigma$ the fitted-oracle arm reports $4\%$ (its three datasets range from $1\%$ to $7\%$) against $10\%$ ($[4, 18]$). From trial 21 the gap closes: the intervals overlap at every magnitude, and at $1\sigma$ the two arms are close ($8\%$ against $10\%$). On the as-logged data the arm reports a third of the search loss, the two affected datasets at $3\%$ and $19\%$ of the floor gap against $24\%$ and $31\%$ on the corrected data. As a share of the achievable improvement, the analytic arm's own unit, the two arms are closer: at $1\sigma$ from the first rating the fitted-oracle arm reports $11.9\%$ (its three datasets range from $6.7\%$ to $16.5\%$) against $14.8\%$ ($[11.4, 18.3]$ from this analysis's own landscape bootstrap; \autoref{tab:onsetbound} gives $[11.5, 18.3]$), a ratio of $1.25$ $[0.84, 2.13]$, with each seed's box mean estimated from the designs its clean random and Sobol runs visited, and the ratio's interval includes $1$ at every magnitude but $0.25\sigma$ ($1.51$ $[1.02, 2.35]$); from trial 21 at $1\sigma$ the two agree ($2.8\%$ against $2.7\%$), and at $5\sigma$ the fitted-oracle arm reports $3.0\%$ against $4.8\%$.

\textbf{On the corrected data the augmentation contrast does not test fidelity.} There, turning off the jitter augmentation changes the oracles' cross-validated $R^2$ by at most $0.03$ (on the as-logged data it raised one by $0.29$), and rerunning the arm without it moves the fraction destroyed by $+0.017$ $[-0.003, +0.037]$ on \texttt{ehmi}, $+0.006$ $[-0.003, +0.016]$ on \texttt{opticarvis} and $-0.014$ $[-0.055, +0.021]$ on \texttt{provoice} ($800$ paired cells each, seed-bootstrap intervals). The isolation experiment below is the test of fidelity. The optimum estimate is not the cause of the gap either: it cancels in the excess, a difference of two regrets, and enters only the denominator, and with each seed's best clean value in its place the ratio of the two arms is $2.0$ $[0.97, 4.0]$; most of the gap comes from the normaliser (above). The three fitted design spaces sit in the lower two thirds of the twenty-landscape spread (per-landscape fractions at $1\sigma$ run from $0.11$ to $4.8$; the fitted three are $0.24$, $0.31$ and $0.75$, above five, six and thirteen of the twenty; as shares of the achievable improvement the twenty run from $0.03$ to $0.33$ and the fitted three are $0.07$, $0.12$ and $0.16$, above three, nine and eleven), and with three design spaces the arm cannot say whether that is a property of human objectives or of these studies.

\textbf{Holding the landscape fixed.} Set against twenty analytic landscapes, three human objectives cannot say whether the gap above belongs to the oracle or to the landscapes. The simulator refits the oracle for every run seed, so each landscape has five oracles, and each normaliser below is formed within each seed's oracle and averaged over seeds before dividing. The error grid and the fidelity figures come from the seed-7 oracle; the other seeds' tree-ensemble oracles have a $\sigma_f$ of $0.94$ to $1.08$ times it, so the nominal magnitudes hold to within $8\%$. The exact arm is the main sweep on the same landscape, acquisitions (LogEI, qNEI, UCB, EI), seeds 7--11 and onset. The two are compared on two normalisers: the achievable improvement, the paper's headline unit ($\optz$ for the exact objective; for a fitted oracle its estimated optimum less its mean over $20{,}000$ uniform points in the box), and the floor gap of \autoref{tab:fitted}. \textbf{The acquisition ranking is too unstable to test the oracle landscape by landscape.} Within every landscape and magnitude the four acquisitions run in both arms (EI, LogEI, UCB, qNEI) were ranked by trajectory loss, in the fitted-oracle arm on seeds 7--11 and in the exact arm on seeds 7--16, both under \textsc{gaussian} error from the first rating (\texttt{scripts/oracle\_isolation\_acq\_ranking.py}); ranks are taken within a cell, so neither arm's normaliser matters. Pooled over the three magnitudes the exact arm's mean ranks are $2.2$ (qNEI), $2.2$ (UCB), $2.8$ (EI) and $2.9$ (LogEI); the fitted-oracle arm's are $2.7$, $2.6$, $2.5$ and $2.3$, a smaller spread in the other direction. The most robust acquisition of a landscape agrees between the arms in $3$, $8$ and $2$ of $20$ cells at $0.25\sigma$, $1\sigma$ and $5\sigma$ (chance is $5$), but the exact arm agrees with itself across disjoint seed halves (7--11 against 12--16) in only $2$, $8$ and $6$ cells ($16$ of $60$) on the trajectory and $17$ of $60$ on the deployed design, so this per-landscape count, on five fitted-arm seeds, has little power to distinguish a fitted oracle from the exact objective. The pooled order is more stable: the exact arm's two seed halves order the four alike on the trajectory (Spearman $0.8$ between their mean ranks), and against it the tree-ensemble oracle's order is reversed ($-0.8$), a reversal that comes from the $0.25\sigma$ cell ($-0.89$), where the exact arm's own halves agree at only $0.0$, and is $-0.6$ against the exact arm on the fitted arm's seeds 7--11; at $1\sigma$, where the halves agree at $0.8$, the tree oracle's order is uncorrelated with the exact one ($0.0$), and at $5\sigma$ it is $0.2$; the GP and MLP oracles keep the order ($1.0$ and $0.8$). On the deployed design the exact arm's own pooled order does not survive a change of seeds ($0.0$ between the halves), so with five seeds per half the pooled order of these four acquisitions cannot be read on the deployed design from either arm. Four acquisitions and twenty landscapes make all of this descriptive.

\textbf{The oracle family changes the reported search loss; no family significantly understates the deployed loss.} The pipeline's model selection offers seven families, all of them tree ensembles, and picks one on every landscape. To see whether what the tree ensembles report belongs to fitted oracles or to tree ensembles, we reran the experiment on the same synthetic datasets with two smooth families forced in their place: a Gaussian process with an ARD RBF kernel and a white-noise term, and a two-layer MLP ($64$ units per layer, early stopping), both on standardised inputs and targets and fitted without jitter augmentation, a tree-ensemble heuristic that would also have made refitting the GP in every run too slow (\texttt{scripts/oracle\_isolation.py --family}, $2{,}400$ runs each). \subsection{The smooth subset, landscape by landscape, and the surrogate's fit}
\label{app:smooth}

The subset was written down before any result was computed, from the landscape definitions alone: the six human-performance laws, of which Yerkes--Dodson and the steering law have one interior peak and Stevens, Hick, Weber--Fechner and the power law of practice are monotone with a corner optimum. Four of them (Yerkes--Dodson, Stevens, Hick, Weber--Fechner) apply the one-dimensional law to each of four inputs and average, so they are additive across inputs; the power law of practice and the steering law map three inputs to the law's parameters and the fourth to its argument. They differ from the classical functions in more than smoothness. All six are four-dimensional, and four of them have their optimum at a corner, which among the classical functions only Eggholder shares, on one face. A secondary split, also fixed in advance, separates the three classical functions that are convex or effectively unimodal on their box (Powell, Rosenbrock, Griewank) from the other ten.

\subsection{Does any of this hold for multiple objectives?}
\label{sec:multiobjective}

The applied setting is frequently multi-objective \citep{daulton2021parallel} and everything above is scalar, so the generalisation cannot be assumed. We repeat the design on seven BoTorch problems with a published maximum hypervolume (BraninCurrin, ZDT1, DTLZ2, DH2, VehicleSafety, Penicillin and CarSideImpact), spanning two to four objectives and two to seven dimensions, with the four hypervolume acquisitions (qEHVI, qNEHVI and their log variants) and the same two model-free floors, under \textsc{gaussian} error, at five seeds: $1{,}680$ paired runs, of which $960$ are model-based noisy-versus-clean comparisons on the problems that survive the headroom screen below.

Standardisation transfers exactly rather than approximately. Each objective is mapped to zero mean and unit variance over a fixed scrambled-Sobol sample of $2^{15}$ points, and under a per-objective affine map the hypervolume scales by $\prod_m \sigma_m$, so the standardised maximum follows in closed form from the published one. We verify this rather than assert it: across the seven problems the largest relative error between the closed-form standardised maximum and a direct recomputation is $4.7 \times 10^{-16}$.

\paragraph{Controls.} The model-free floor is again identically zero across all $560$ such comparisons, so no observation noise leaks into the candidate stream in the hypervolume code path either. No run beats its published maximum hypervolume. Acquisition-optimization fallbacks total zero, which we report rather than assume: a hypervolume acquisition that fails to construct falls back to random sampling, and because that produces a plausible-looking curve rather than an error, the arm has to be read off the fallback counter and not off the plots.

\paragraph{One problem is dropped by the headroom screen.} DH2's reference point leaves a $32{,}768$-point Sobol sample with a hypervolume of exactly zero, and its best learner does not improve on the model-free floor at all. There is nothing for error to destroy, so its null is guaranteed by construction. It is excluded by the multi-objective arm's headroom screen of $0.15$, stricter than the scalar suite's $0.10$, which DH2's headroom of $0.0$ fails either way. The remaining six pass, with Penicillin weakest at $0.197$, comparable to Rosenbrock at $0.176$, the weakest admissible scalar landscape.

\paragraph{The comparison needs one piece of care.} Elsewhere the paper divides excess regret by $\optz$, a z-score of the optimum against a random design's mean. A Pareto problem has no scalar optimum and therefore no $\optz$, and the natural hypervolume analogue, the gap between the published maximum and what a model-free design of the same budget reaches, is a numerically much smaller denominator. Quoting one arm's published fraction against the other's would manufacture a threefold difference out of the choice of divisor alone. Both columns of \autoref{tab:mo} are therefore recomputed on the floor denominator, identically; and we also report the onset ratio, a ratio of two costs on the same problem. On one problem it does not depend on the denominator; pooled over problems it does, through the weight each problem receives, and the scalar arm's is $9.6\times$ $[5.6, 14.4]$ at $1\sigma$ on the floor gap and $5.5\times$ $[3.8, 9.0]$ on $\optz$ (post-onset search loss, \textsc{gaussian} error).

\textbf{The two headline structures replicate.} Cost rises monotonically with error magnitude and falls sharply when the error arrives late, on both arms. At $1\sigma$ from the first rating the multi-objective arm loses $49.1\%$ of the hypervolume floor gap $[42, 56]$ against the scalar arm's $99.0\%$ $[54, 154]$ (this analysis's own landscape bootstrap; Appendix~\ref{sec:fitted} gives $[55, 155]$); arriving at trial 21 the two are indistinguishable, $10.7\%$ $[2, 24]$ against $10.3\%$ $[7, 13]$.

\textbf{On the trajectory, neither arm is clearly the more fragile of the two.} On the floor gap, the point estimates for error from the first rating are half the scalar arm's or less at every magnitude, a seventh at $0.05\sigma$, a third at $0.25\sigma$ and half at $1\sigma$ and $5\sigma$, and the intervals separate at $0.05\sigma$ and $0.25\sigma$; at $1\sigma$ they overlap slightly and at $5\sigma$ substantially, so this is a consistent direction rather than a clean separation at the top of the range. The onset ratio is flatter, $3.0$--$5.5\times$ across magnitudes against the scalar arm's $3.6$--$9.6\times$, and the difference is largely mechanical. On the same floor-gap basis, the running-maximum bound of Appendix~\ref{app:onsetbound}, the clean run's improvement after the prefix it shares with the noisy run, is $7.0\times$ $[2.6, 25]$ larger for error from the first rating than from trial 21 on the multi-objective arm, against $12.2\times$ $[6.7, 18]$ on the scalar arm, because the clean hypervolume run has relatively more improvement left to make after trial 21. Divided by that bound, the onset ratio is $0.65\times$ $[0.54, 1.01]$ against $0.79\times$ $[0.66, 1.00]$ at $1\sigma$ and $0.79\times$ $[0.67, 0.98]$ against $0.74\times$ $[0.69, 0.85]$ at $5\sigma$ (post-onset search loss, \textsc{gaussian} error), so on a log scale the bound accounts for about three quarters of the gap between the two arms' onset ratios at $1\sigma$ and all of it at $5\sigma$. The lower level from the first rating is the bound's as well: from the first rating the bound averages $106\%$ $[77, 132]$ of the floor gap on the multi-objective arm and $274\%$ $[131, 454]$ on the scalar arm, and the error destroys $46\%$ $[36, 61]$ of it against $36\%$ $[33, 43]$ at $1\sigma$ and $68\%$ $[53, 81]$ against $54\%$ $[50, 62]$ at $5\sigma$. Neither observation requires the hypervolume search to be more robust. The front the study would \emph{report} fares worse than the search: at $1\sigma$ from the first rating about half of it is truly dominated ($5.1$ of $10.9$ designs), it holds $29\%$ of the truly non-dominated designs evaluated, and its hypervolume shortfall exceeds that of the evaluated set in all eight cells.

\textbf{The choice among hypervolume acquisitions does not appear to matter.} Mean ranks span $2.40$ to $2.60$ on a scale where $2.5$ is a tie, absolute losses span $20.2\%$ to $20.8\%$, and the Friedman test reaches $p < 0.05$ in $0$ of $8$ conditions (\autoref{tab:moacq}). A hypervolume analogue would have to define the one-shot loss over hypervolume contributions and take a stand on how per-objective error propagates into them. \autoref{tab:mediation} is not claimed to extend to it.

\subsection{Re-scoring the design a study ships}
\label{app:shiprules}

The deployed regret everywhere else in this paper uses one ship rule: the design with the single best observed rating. When several visited designs share that rating, the rule ships the one visited first, as \texttt{argmax} does; every other re-scoring and replay in this paper reproduces the logged choice and so keeps that convention. The last paragraph of this subsection says where it matters and gives the values under a uniformly random choice among the tied designs. To separate search from selection (Section~\ref{sec:decomp}) we re-score what the same trials would have shipped under other rules, adding no trial. The loop's own surrogate, BoTorch's default single-task GP, normalised over the landscape's box and standardised, is refitted on every logged observation, and each rule picks among the \emph{visited} designs: the best observed rating (the standard rule), the best mean rating, the posterior-mean maximiser, the maximiser of the posterior mean less one or two latent SDs, and, as the ceiling, the visited design with the best true value. The arm's own logged rule must reproduce the logged final regret to $10^{-9}$ in every run or the analysis stops; over the $66{,}000$ runs it did, with no failed fits.

Scored as a share of the deployed loss of \textsc{gaussian} error, the cautious rule (posterior mean less two latent SDs) recovers $+5.8\%$ $[2, 10]$ at $1\sigma$ from the first rating, $+12.6\%$ $[4, 23]$ at $1\sigma$ from trial 21, $+9.7\%$ $[5, 15]$ and $+18.5\%$ $[11, 26]$ at $5\sigma$. It pays a fixed price of $0.015$ of the achievable improvement whether or not error is present. At $0.05\sigma$ it therefore costs $81$--$147\%$ more than the error does, and at $0.25\sigma$ it still does not pay ($-1.9\%$ $[-15, +7]$ from the first rating, $-12.8\%$ $[-45, +11]$ from trial 21), so its break-even lies between $0.25\sigma$ and $1\sigma$ and it should not be used without an estimate of the rater's noise. Pooled over $0.25\sigma$ and above it recovers $10.1\%$ $[4.6, 15.9]$, but the $5\sigma$ cells carry two thirds ($66.5\%$) of that pool's cost. At $1\sigma$ over both onsets it recovers $7.7\%$ $[2.7, 12.8]$, and pooled over $0.25\sigma$ and $1\sigma$ alone $4.6\%$ $[-2.9, 11.2]$. Under \textsc{bias} it survives correction at $1\sigma$ and $5\sigma$ from the first rating ($+7.1\%$ $[3, 11]$, $+9.0\%$ $[5, 14]$) and loses at $5\sigma$ from trial 21 ($-7.7\%$ $[-12, -4]$); under \textsc{drift} only $5\sigma$ from the first rating survives ($+4.0\%$ $[2, 7]$), and under \textsc{ar}(1) nothing does.

The tie convention matters mainly where the rating scale is bounded or discrete. In the main sweep the top rating is tied only between exact ratings, in a clean twin or before an onset at trial 21, so the tied designs have the same true value: none of the $76{,}800$ noisy runs has a tie between designs of different true value, and neither do the unclipped gross-fault arms, the rater-offset arms or the fitted-oracle arms. A capped, clipped or rounded scale ties the top rating between designs of different true value in $99.5\%$ of the runs under the fixed cap, $52$--$77\%$ of the clipped runs and $61\%$ of the rounded instrument's. Under the fixed cap and under clipping the earliest tied design is worse than the average tied design in $70\%$ and $53$--$69\%$ of those runs, so there the convention adds to the measured cost. Scored instead as the exact expectation over a uniformly random choice among the tied designs, from the same logs (\texttt{scripts/review\_checks/tie\_break.py}), the capped scale's extra cost on the deployed design falls from $185\%$ $[132, 258]$ to $135\%$ $[92, 196]$, the re-anchored cap's recovery from $25\%$ $[17, 32]$ to $18\%$ $[9, 25]$, and the rank refit's under the cap from $36\%$ $[29, 43]$ to $23\%$ $[17, 28]$ ($38\%$ to $21\%$ on seeds 12--16). Clipping's recovery rises, from $22\%$ $[4, 38]$ to $44\%$ $[31, 55]$ at $15\%$ of trials spiking at $20$ SD and from $35\%$, $22\%$ and $6\%$ to $42\%$, $27\%$ and $24\%$ in the other three settings. Each share in the capped-scale column of \autoref{tab:shortlist} falls by about $10$ to $15$ points, and \autoref{tab:remedies}, \autoref{tab:heldout}, \autoref{tab:budgetneutral} and Appendices~\ref{app:budgetneutral} and~\ref{app:remedies} give both values. In the slip arms at most $5\%$ of runs have a tie between designs of different true value; no cell's deployed excess moves by more than half a point of the achievable improvement, and no slip row of \autoref{tab:adapt}, \autoref{tab:budgetneutral} or \autoref{tab:remedysplit} by more than half a point. \autoref{tab:instrument} reports the trajectory loss, which does not depend on the ship rule. The rating loop of the comparison arm (Appendix~\ref{app:elicitation}) also ships the earliest design at its cap, and that appendix gives both conventions.

\subsection{Predicting the cost from one noisy choice}\label{app:mediation}
Predicting algorithm behaviour from cheap landscape statistics has a long history in evolutionary computation \citep{weinberger1990correlated, jones1995fitness, malan2013survey}; the descriptors of \autoref{tab:benchmarks} are of that kind. The predictor below is a one-trial version of the selection loss itself.



\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/frag_scatter.pdf}
\caption{The one-shot selection loss $\frag(\sigma_e)$ of a landscape (\autoref{eq:frag}, computed on 256 quasi-random designs with no optimizer) against the trajectory loss the sweep measured on it, one point per landscape and magnitude, error from the first rating. Shape and shade both encode the magnitude. Nine of the eighty cells cannot be placed on log axes, eight because $\frag$ is exactly zero and one because the measured cost is negative. Both correlations in the panel are over all eighty. The pooled value largely restates that both quantities grow with $\sigma_e$, which is why the within-magnitude range is given beside it.}
\label{fig:frag}
\end{figure}

Define $\frag(\sigma_e)$ as the expected loss from a single greedy choice among $m$ quasi-random candidates when each is observed with $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_e^2)$ error:
\begin{equation}
  \frag(\sigma_e) \;=\;
  \mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\Big[\max_i g_i - g_{\arg\max_i (g_i + \epsilon_i)}\Big],
  \qquad \epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_e^2),
  \label{eq:frag}
\end{equation}
where $g_i = g(x_i)$ over a fixed Sobol design. It involves no surrogate, no sequence and no acquisition function, and at $m = 256$ it costs about $0.2$ seconds per landscape. It is the expected opportunity cost of one noisy selection in the ranking-and-selection literature \citep{kim2006selecting}, applied to a random design, and it is the selection term of \autoref{eq:decomp} for a study of one trial per design and no search.

Jointly they reach $R^2 = 0.70$, and in that additive model the error magnitude collapses to $\beta = -0.07$ with $p = 0.44$. Refitted on the same post-onset excess as a fraction of $\optz$ (the trajectory loss), the rows without $\optz$ give $R^2 = 0.22$ for the indicators, $0.51$ with the magnitude, $0.46$ with $\frag$ and $0.54$ with the magnitude and $\frag$ together, against $0.04$, $0.16$, $0.52$ and $0.54$ in landscape SDs (the last is not a row of \autoref{tab:mediation}, whose ``both'' row adds the descriptors). On the fraction of $\optz$ the magnitude alone predicts more than $\frag$ alone, so $\frag$'s lead over the magnitude is a property of the landscape-SD scale, and $\frag$ adds to the magnitude only while it is left in landscape SDs ($\beta = +0.022$ $[0.007, 0.066]$); divided by $\optz$ like the response, it adds nothing ($\beta = +0.002$ $[-0.018, +0.021]$, $R^2 = 0.51$). Dimension and ruggedness are not detectably different from zero in the landscape-SD models of \autoref{tab:mediation} (intervals $[-0.26, 0.12]$ and $[-0.34, 0.15]$), though the fixed-$\optz$ ladder of Appendix~\ref{sec:manipulation} shows dimension does raise the cost.

\subsection{Does the surrogate know it is being lied to?}
\label{sec:noisediag}
The GP fits its observation noise as a free hyperparameter under a prior that shrinks it, so a measured cost of error could in principle be the surrogate's failure to notice the error rather than the information the error destroys. This is the first thing to rule out, because if it were the explanation then every number above would be a statement about BoTorch's default prior rather than about noisy feedback.

The mis-estimation is real, and it lands where it would do the most damage (\autoref{tab:noisediag}). Refitting each surrogate from the sweep's own logs using only its first $n$ observations, at $1\sigma$ and above the fitted noise standard deviation is about an eighth of the injected one over the first fifteen of fifty observations, $0.56$--$0.66$ of it by the twenty-fifth and $0.83$--$0.89$ by the fiftieth, while at $0.05\sigma$ it is overestimated by a quarter to a half throughout, so a diagnostic evaluated only at $n = 50$ looks reassuring and is misleading. Refitting with a wide noise prior recovers the injected level far sooner ($0.55$ rather than $0.14$ at $n = 15$, $1\sigma$), so the shrinkage is the prior's doing and not a limit of the data.

It was natural to expect this to explain the onset effect of Section~\ref{sec:cost}: from the first rating the error arrives in exactly the window where the noise cannot yet be estimated, whereas at a mid-run onset the surrogate has a settled lengthscale and thirty trials in which to learn. \textbf{We tested that directly and it is wrong.} Supplying the true injected variance as \texttt{train\_Yvar} and re-running $4{,}800$ paired cells removes about $3\%$ of the summed post-onset trajectory loss of the eight cells in \autoref{tab:knownnoise}, each arm measured against its own clean run ($+15\%$ at $0.25\sigma$ from the first rating, $-1\%$ and $+4\%$ at $1\sigma$ and $5\sigma$, and $+1\%$ and $+2\%$ at $1\sigma$ and $5\sigma$ from trial 21; the other three cells have reference losses below $0.01$ of the achievable improvement that are negative on at least one landscape, and there the change is $-0.0003$ and $+0.0015$ at $0.05\sigma$ from the first rating and from trial 21 and $-0.0018$ at $0.25\sigma$ from trial 21; against the standard process the arm recovers $-3\%$ of the trajectory loss at a price of $0.4$ points and $-3\%$ of the deployed loss at none, Appendix~\ref{app:adapt}), no condition survives false-discovery-rate correction, and the onset ratio at $1\sigma$ does not shrink: it is $5.1$ before and $5.1$ after. Bayesian optimization is insensitive to its own noise hyperparameter in this regime, despite getting it badly wrong for the first fifteen trials, worth knowing on its own, and a caution against reading too much into noise-estimation diagnostics. It is still measured through one surrogate, BoTorch's default RBF GP, which reads the roughness of the ten rugged classical functions as noise (Appendix~\ref{app:smooth}), so \autoref{tab:dose} describes each landscape as that surrogate searches it. The trajectory onset effect is the clean run's post-onset improvement, the bound of Appendix~\ref{app:onsetbound}, not the surrogate's noise estimate.

\subsection{Breaking the descriptor confound}
\label{sec:manipulation}
In our suite $\optz$, sparsity and skew correlate at about $0.9$, and for a bounded function with an isolated optimum that is close to a structural identity, so Appendix~\ref{app:mediation}'s descriptor terms cannot be separated observationally, however many real landscapes are added. Two purpose-built families vary them by construction: a cos-field background plus one spike, whose amplitude moves $\optz$ and whose width moves sparsity; and the Levy function at $d \in \{4, 7, 11\}$, one landscape family across a dimension ladder (\autoref{tab:manipulation}).

\textbf{$\optz$ on its own is not causal.} Holding the spike narrow and raising its amplitude by $4\times$ moves $\optz$ from $5.0$ to $21.7$ and changes the excess regret at $1\sigma$ from $0.246$ to $0.245$, a paired difference of $-0.001$ $[-0.035, +0.036]$ (seed bootstrap, five seeds). At the other magnitudes the change is not zero but never significantly upward: $+0.007$ $[-0.012, +0.018]$ at $0.05\sigma$, $-0.127$ $[-0.358, +0.004]$ at $0.25\sigma$ and $-0.062$ $[-0.125, -0.012]$ at $5\sigma$. Widen the spike and the same amplitude change raises excess from $0.495$ to $1.888$ at $1\sigma$ ($+1.39$ $[+0.26, +2.53]$), and at every magnitude from $0.25\sigma$. What the observational $\beta_{\optz}$ picks up ($+0.42$ in the joint $\frag$-plus-descriptor model, $+0.64$ with the descriptors alone; \autoref{tab:mediation}) is therefore an interaction, not a main effect: a taller optimum costs more to miss only when it is broad enough that the optimizer would otherwise have found it. On a true needle, the optimizer was never going to find the optimum with or without noise, so there is nothing for the noise to destroy. It also means that the $\optz$ exponent of Appendix~\ref{sec:currency} describes the suite rather than a mechanism: it is not $\optz$ as such that makes an error expensive.

\textbf{Dimension, and a within-family ladder that corrects itself.} Within the Levy family, excess regret at $1\sigma$ rises monotonically from $0.114$ at $d = 4$ to $0.222$ at $d = 7$ and $0.264$ at $d = 11$, while the cross-suite regression puts $\beta_d$ at $-0.03$ and not significant. Read on its own that looks like a real effect the observational analysis missed. It is not, or not mostly: $\optz$ also rises along the Levy ladder, $1.53$ to $2.07$ to $2.62$, so that comparison varies dimension and signal strength together.

The \texttt{bump} ladder separates them. Amplitude and width are chosen at each dimension so that $\optz$ is $9.0$ and the spike occupies a $10^{-6}$ fraction of the box at all three, leaving dimension as the only difference. In the table's units, excess regret is $0.28$ at $d = 4$, $0.25$ at $d = 7$ and $0.43$ at $d = 11$. The $d = 4$ rung has headroom $0.098$, just below the $0.10$ screen applied to every scalar landscape, so the comparison that clears the screen is $d = 7$ to $11$: there the fixed-$\optz$ ladder rises $1.7\times$ $[1.1, 5.3]$ at $1\sigma$ and $1.7\times$ $[1.4, 2.3]$ at $5\sigma$ (ten seeds, paired), where Levy rises $1.2\times$ $[0.6, 1.9]$; at $0.25\sigma$ the interval, $[0.6, 3.7]$, includes no change, and at $1\sigma$ the excluded $d = 4$ rung lies above $d = 7$. \textbf{With $\optz$ held fixed, dimension raises the cost at $1\sigma$ and $5\sigma$ on a two-point comparison. What the Levy ladder cannot say is how much of its $2.3\times$ is dimension rather than signal strength.} What rises monotonically with dimension on both ladders is the inference metric, the true value of the design a practitioner would deploy: $0.52$, $0.70$, $0.99$ on the fixed-$\optz$ ladder. Higher dimension makes the progress harder to identify from noisy observations at the end as well as harder to make.

The methodological lesson is that a within-family ladder is not automatically a manipulation of the property it is indexed by: the Levy ladder varied signal strength along with dimension, and ours became a manipulation of dimension only when it was built to hold $\optz$ fixed.

\subsection{The input-error arm}
\label{app:inputerrorarm}

Every error process above corrupts the reported value. People also make the other mistake: a slider overshoots, the wrong option is pressed, a configuration is applied to the wrong condition. The optimizer proposes $x$, the person experiences $x' \neq x$, rates it honestly, and the rating is filed against $x$: the right value at the wrong location. A fitted oracle cannot score it, since that needs the objective at $x'$.

A \textsc{slip} displaces every trial by $\delta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, s^2)$ per coordinate, with $s$ a fraction of that coordinate's range, and clips the result to the box. A \textsc{misclick} leaves the trial exact with probability $1 - p$ and otherwise lands on a uniformly random design. Both magnitudes are swept over $\{0.01, 0.05, 0.15, 0.4\}$ at the same onsets and budget, with six model-based acquisitions (LogEI, EI, PI, UCB, qUCB, qNEI), the two floors and five seeds. Neither is in landscape SDs, so the comparison with the response-error grid is on the damage axis only.

If the slip goes unnoticed the surrogate trains on $(x, g(x'))$ and the deployed design is the recorded $x$, so we score it there. A counterfactual arm records $(x', g(x'))$, as an instrumented interface would, with identical slip draws. The contrast separates the cost of evaluating the wrong point from that of mislabelling it. The model-free floors are not a zero control here: they evaluate the wrong points without learning, so their excess is the geometric cost of a slip alone.

\textbf{A slip costs about as much as a rating error.} A slip of $5\%$ of each coordinate's range on every trial, from the first, destroys $5.4\%$ of the achievable improvement; one of $15\%$ destroys $11.5\%$; one of $40\%$, $23.2\%$ (\autoref{tab:inputerror}). On this arm's six acquisitions and seeds under \textsc{gaussian} error, the $0.25\sigma$, $1\sigma$ and $5\sigma$ rating errors of \autoref{tab:dose} cost $3.8\%$, $11.7\%$ and $21.3\%$ (the $T=50$ row of \autoref{tab:budget}; pooled: $5.3\%$, $14.1\%$, $22.0\%$): a $5\%$ slip costs more than a $0.25\sigma$ rating error, a $15\%$ slip about as much as $1\sigma$, a $40\%$ slip about as much as $5\sigma$. A misclick is cheaper at every matched magnitude: even when $40\%$ of trials land somewhere random it costs $13.5\%$, little more than a $15\%$ slip on every trial and a little over half of a $40\%$ one. A late slip is cheaper than an early one by less than a late rating error: $1.4$--$3.6\times$ against $5.1\times$ at $1\sigma$ on the same basis.

\textbf{Half of a small slip's cost is the record, not the displacement.} The decomposition in \autoref{tab:inputerror} (in full, \autoref{tab:inputmech}) separates the mechanisms. The model-free floor barely moves, since a random design displaced is still random; only at $40\%$, where clipping piles evaluations at the box edges, does it lose $6.4\%$ $[0, 13]$. A learner with a correct record loses far more, $2.8\%$ at a $5\%$ slip and $19.6\%$ at $40\%$: a displaced proposal is a worse one. Mislabelling adds $2.7$ points at $5\%$ and $4.4$ at $15\%$, half and two-fifths of the total (paired intervals $[0.35, 0.61]$ and $[0.26, 0.49]$; not estimable at $1\%$); at $40\%$ the displacement dominates and the mislabelled share falls to a sixth.

\textbf{The design in the log is not the design that earned its rating.} Scored at the recorded $x$ a practitioner would deploy, the final design's excess regret is $16.4\%$ of the achievable improvement at a $5\%$ slip from the first trial, $34.3\%$ at $15\%$ and $65.8\%$ at $40\%$, against $6.5\%$, $13.4\%$ and $24.9\%$ for the best design the person experienced (Appendix~\ref{app:inputerror}): a factor of $2.5$ to $2.6$, and $1.6$ at a $1\%$ slip. The optimizer's progress survives an input error far better than the record of it, which is what a deployment inherits.

\subsection{End-of-study procedures}
\label{app:endofstudy}

Both procedures keep $T$ fixed and spend the final trials differently, so each is replayed exactly from an existing log: truncate at the trial the procedure starts, refit the loop's GP on that prefix, and simulate only the new trials. The premise is checked against the re-rating arm, whose earlier trials must equal the standard run's.

\emph{Sitting($k$)}, the tournament of the code. Search trials $1..T-k$. The last $k$ are one sitting in which each of $k$ candidates is shown once. Candidates are the top $k$ visited designs by the posterior mean less one latent SD, or by observed rating. The winner is the best look, or the posterior mean after conditioning on the looks. The sitting is modelled as simultaneous: the bias, the \textsc{drift} ramp and the \textsc{ar}(1) state are held fixed across its looks, so they cancel under comparison and only the idiosyncratic part enters: $\sigma$ for \textsc{gaussian}, \textsc{bias} and \textsc{drift}, the innovation for \textsc{ar}(1). That is the model everywhere in the paper, and the last paragraph of this section relaxes it. The replay also ran the looks at half the idiosyncratic SD, a sensitivity for which we can name no mechanism: a comparison between two looks carries the noise of both, $\sqrt2$ times one rating's (Appendix~\ref{app:elicitation}), not half of it. At half the SD the sitting over the top five recovers $19.7\%$ $[13, 27]$ of \textsc{gaussian} error's deployed loss rather than $12.2\%$, and the fixed $k=16$ gains $+0.051$ rather than $+0.022$. Every headline figure in the paper is at the full SD. At the full SD the sitting over the top five recovers $26.3\%$ of the deployed loss at $5\sigma$ from trial 21 and $23.8\%$ $[16, 31]$ under an unnoticed slip, at a price of $0.024$. A slip or a misclick leaves the rating itself exact, so in those arms the looks carry an assumed rating-noise SD of $0.25$ landscape SDs in the noisy and the clean twin alike. At an SD of $0$, $0.1$ and $0.5$ the same sitting recovers $31.9\%$ $[24, 41]$, $28.8\%$ $[21, 37]$ and $17.8\%$ $[10, 25]$ of the slip's deployed loss, at prices of $0.012$, $0.017$ and $0.030$. Under misclicks, whose cost is small ($0.068$), it gains $-0.002$, $-0.008$ and $-0.026$ of the achievable improvement at those SDs, against $-0.017$ at $0.25$, at the same prices.

\emph{Confirmation($k$)}. Search trials $1..T-2k$. The last $2k$ rate the chosen design and the best initial design $k$ times each in counterbalanced order. The chosen design ships if its \emph{mean} rating over those $k$ is at least the initial design's, and the study \emph{claims} an improvement only if a one-sided paired $t$-test at $\alpha=0.05$ prefers it. The two decisions are separate, which is why this changes little about the design that ships (it recovers $3.2\%$ and $3.4\%$ of the deployed cost, intervals $[-5, 12]$, at a price of $0.021$ with four trials and $0.032$ with eight) and a great deal about what the study may claim. The standard process claims an improvement in $98.3\%$ of runs and that claim is false in $15.7\%$ of them, rising to $42.7\%$ at $5\sigma$ from the first rating. Four confirmation trials take the false-claim rate to $0.5\%$ overall and $1.0\%$ at $5\sigma$ from the first rating, at the cost of detecting only $50.7\%$ of the real improvements ($25.9\%$ at $1\sigma$ from the first rating); eight trials give $0.46\%$ and $65.7\%$. Two changes are bundled there, and they are worth separating, because the standard process applies no test at all: it claims whenever the best observed rating of the run exceeds the best of the initial design, which is true unless the two coincide, so its power is $1.000$ and its size $0.75$--$1.00$ in the \textsc{gaussian} cells. Claiming on the fresh mean alone, with the trials spent but no test, already takes the false-claim rate from $15.7\%$ to $6.6\%$. The $\alpha = 0.05$ test takes it from $6.6\%$ to $0.5\%$. Of the $15.2$-point reduction the trials therefore buy $9.1$ points, $60\%$, and the test $6.1$. The test is not free: claiming on the fresh mean detects $88\%$ of the real improvements, and adding the test cuts that to $51\%$. Power is also strongly magnitude-dependent: $87.5\%$ at $0.05\sigma$ and $6.8\%$ at $5\sigma$ from the first rating, so the pooled $50.7\%$ is carried by the cells where little is at stake. All figures in this paragraph are the \textsc{gaussian} cells.

\emph{The sitting as a sequence of trials.} Under the per-trial definitions of Section~\ref{sec:errors} the \textsc{drift} ramp and the \textsc{ar}(1) state move during a long sitting: at $5\sigma$ from trial 21 the ramp rises by $2.5$ landscape SDs over a sitting of sixteen, and two \textsc{ar}(1) looks $L$ trials apart differ with variance $2\sigma^2(1 - 0.8^L)$, $1.93\sigma^2$ at $L = 15$, against $0.72\sigma^2$ in the simultaneous model. A sequential replay continues both through trials $T-k+1$ to $T$, the \textsc{ar}(1) state from the run's own logged error at trial $T-k$, and shows the candidates in an order drawn at random per run; \textsc{gaussian} and \textsc{bias} are unchanged by construction. \texttt{scripts/review\_checks/sitting\_sequential.py} re-scores with it every pooled gain of the fixed-allocation sitting that the paper reports, by the producers of \autoref{tab:sittingcell}, \autoref{fig:kcurve} and \autoref{tab:heldout}. None moves by more than $0.007$ of the achievable improvement; the largest move is the sitting of twenty-five at $1\sigma$ from the first rating in \autoref{fig:kcurve}, from $-0.077$ to $-0.084$. On seeds 12--16 the sitting of sixteen at $5\sigma$ from trial 21 gains $+0.226$ $[0.142, 0.320]$ rather than $+0.227$ $[0.147, 0.321]$ (\textsc{drift} $+0.327$ rather than $+0.346$, \textsc{ar}(1) $+0.258$ rather than $+0.244$, and \textsc{gaussian} $+0.210$ $[0.099, 0.348]$ under either model), and the sitting of two at $1\sigma$ from the first rating gains $+0.031$ $[0.013, 0.051]$ rather than $+0.030$ (Holm $p = 0.02$ rather than $0.03$). On all seeds the pooled gains of $k = 5$ to $16$ stay between $+0.020$ and $+0.022$, and at half the idiosyncratic SD the fixed $k = 16$ gains $+0.049$ rather than $+0.051$. On the held-out seeds the derived budget rule gains $-0.007$ $[-0.022, +0.006]$, $0.025$ rather than $0.024$ below the pooled $k = 12$. Four of the eighteen choices of $k$ in \autoref{tab:sittingcell} change, each to a size that led the old one by at most $0.002$ on the seeds that chose it: on seeds 7--11 from $5$ to $3$ at $0.25\sigma$ with either onset, where every size loses, and on seeds 12--16 from $5$ to $3$ at $1\sigma$ from trial 21 and from $5$ to $2$ at $5\sigma$ from the first rating. Over the $41$ procedures of Appendix~\ref{app:heldout} the loss of the sitting of thirty is then no longer significant after Holm's correction (adjusted $p = 0.06$ rather than $0.03$); the best-arm sittings keep their simultaneous-model values in that family. Showing the candidates best-ranked first, so that a rising ramp favours the lower-ranked ones, gives $+0.212$ $[0.128, 0.305]$ and $+0.031$ $[0.013, 0.051]$ and pooled gains of $+0.018$ to $+0.020$. The largest move is then $0.015$, in the sitting of sixteen at $5\sigma$ from trial 21, the loss of the sitting of thirty stays significant (adjusted $p = 0.008$), and four choices of $k$ change, among them the $k$ chosen on seeds 7--11 at $1\sigma$ from trial 21, from $16$ to $3$, and the pooled $k$ chosen on seeds 12--16, from $8$ to $5$. Confirmation's counterbalanced trials follow the same process, which moves its four-trial false-claim rate under \textsc{ar}(1) from $0.50\%$ to $0.62\%$ and leaves the \textsc{gaussian} figures above unchanged. The sensitivity covers the fixed-allocation sitting and confirmation; the best-arm sitting of Appendix~\ref{app:remedies} was replayed under the simultaneous model only, and order effects beyond the simulated processes, such as fatigue beyond the drift ramp or contrast and anchoring between consecutive designs, are not modelled.

\subsection{Deriving the split from the run in hand}
\label{app:budgetrule}

Write $\mu_i, \sigma_i$ for the loop GP's posterior latent mean and SD at the distinct designs visited by the decision point, ordered by the posterior mean less one latent SD, and $s$ for the idiosyncratic rating SD of a sitting. A sitting of $k$ shows the top $k$ candidates once each, so with $f_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2)$ and looks $y_i = f_i + \mathcal{N}(0, s^2)$ the identification term is $U(k) = \mathbb{E}[f_{\arg\max_i y_i}]$, evaluated by Monte Carlo. It is not monotone in $k$: a larger candidate set is more likely to contain the best visited design and more likely to let a worse one win on a lucky look. The search term prices the forgone trials by the run's own gain in best rating per trial over the last ten, floored at zero. Every policy (ship by posterior mean, ship by the posterior mean less one latent SD, or a sitting of $k$) is scored under the single posterior at $n_0 = T - \max k$, the last trial at which all of them are still open; scoring each $k$ under its own $T-k$ posterior looks more faithful and is not, because two GPs fitted on different numbers of points put their maxima on different scales, and the comparison then picks up that difference rather than the difference between policies. With the grid of Section~\ref{sec:budget}, $\max k = 30$ of $T = 50$, the decision point is trial 20, so under error from trial 21 the rule decides on twenty exact ratings. In every such run its fit, its sitting SD and its scores for the two no-trial rules are identical at all four magnitudes, and its choice is the same in $86\%$ of runs, the rest differing only in Monte Carlo draws. The sitting's SD is read from the surrogate's fitted noise, which is the noise of the \emph{standardised} targets, and multiplied by the outcome transform's scale so that it and the posterior sit on one axis; without that conversion a sitting would appear up to $5.6$ times more precise than it is. The rule reads only ratings and the surrogate. The true objective enters only when the choice is scored, against the realised regret of that $(\text{run}, k)$ in the replay.

\textbf{The rule by onset.} Unless stated otherwise, every gain in this paragraph and the next is the deployed design's gain over the standard process at $T$, in units of the achievable improvement, on all ten seeds, for LogEI and qNEI under the four processes with looks at the full idiosyncratic SD. A difference between two rules is a difference of such gains, paired within landscapes before the bootstrap, and a rule's price is the regret it adds to the deployed design of the clean twins, with the rule derived on them (\texttt{scripts/budget\_split.py}, \texttt{scripts/review\_checks/budget\_split\_by\_onset.py}). Under error from the first rating the rule gains $+0.004$ $[-0.014, +0.022]$, level with $k = 12$ (difference $+0.001$ $[-0.014, +0.014]$) and $0.009$ $[-0.002, +0.021]$ below $k = 8$. From trial 21 it gains $-0.014$ $[-0.032, +0.004]$, $0.052$ $[0.034, 0.070]$ below $k = 12$, most of it in the $5\sigma$ cell, where $k = 12$ gains $+0.163$ and the rule $-0.018$; without that cell it is $0.003$ $[-0.009, +0.017]$ below $k = 12$ over both onsets. Its price is $0.025$ $[0.008, 0.047]$, against $0.015$ for $k = 8$ and $0.022$ for $k = 12$. Both no-trial options are scored by the posterior mean of the design each would ship, so shipping the posterior-mean design always wins that comparison and the rule never ships by the posterior mean less one latent SD. It ships the posterior-mean design in $74\%$ of runs and in $78\%$ of the clean twins, beats always doing so by $0.008$ $[0.003, 0.014]$, and trails always shipping by the posterior mean less one latent SD by $0.006$ $[0.002, 0.009]$. On the held-out seeds, making that swap wherever it chose the posterior-mean design would close $0.007$ of its shortfall to $k = 12$, leaving $0.017$ $[0.003, 0.031]$. From trial 21 a misspecified posterior is not needed to explain the shortfall. It is $0.036$ $[0.013, 0.063]$ on the six laws, where the GP fits, and $0.054$ $[0.023, 0.080]$ on the ten rugged classical functions, and across the twenty landscapes its Spearman correlation with the GP's $R^2$ at fresh points and with its leave-one-out fit is $-0.07$ and $-0.10$. From the first rating, where the rule can see the error, the evidence is mixed. On the six laws the rule gains $0.011$ $[-0.004, +0.025]$ more than $k = 8$ and $0.023$ $[0.010, 0.035]$ more than $k = 12$; on the ten rugged functions it gains $0.019$ $[0.001, 0.038]$ less than $k = 8$ and $0.010$ $[-0.012, +0.033]$ less than $k = 12$, and on the other four $0.015$ $[0.003, 0.028]$ and $0.006$ $[-0.008, +0.016]$ less. That is the pattern misspecification predicts, but across landscapes the rule's gain less $k = 8$ correlates with the GP's $R^2$ at fresh points at only $+0.37$ ($p = 0.11$), and none of the eight correlations of its gain less $k = 8$ or $k = 12$, at either onset, with either measure of fit has $p < 0.1$ (they run from $-0.10$ to $+0.37$). With six, ten and four landscapes the group intervals are rough.

\textbf{Deciding after the onset.} Capping the grid at $k = 25$ moves the single decision to trial 25, five ratings into the late onset, which only an experimenter who knew the onset could arrange. It does not help: from trial 21 the rule then gains $-0.017$ $[-0.033, -0.002]$, $0.055$ $[0.035, 0.073]$ below $k = 12$, and ships the posterior-mean design more often the larger the error ($78\%$ of runs at $0.05\sigma$, $93\%$ at $5\sigma$). The sequential form decides at each $n_j = T - k_j$, from the largest $k$ down. It scores the options still open, both no-trial options and every sitting of $k \le k_j$, under the one posterior at $n_j$, commits to the sitting of $k_j$ if that scores best and otherwise searches on, and its choice at the smallest $k$ is final. Every comparison stays within one posterior and the rule needs no knowledge of the onset, so this is the coherent recomputation. We built it after seeing the by-onset result on all ten seeds, so seeds 12--16 are not held out for it and it is in neither Holm family of Appendix~\ref{app:heldout}; it adds no parameter to the decide-once rule. Under error from trial 21 it commits before rating any trial with error in $0.8\%$ of runs. Pooled over both onsets it gains $+0.009$ $[-0.003, +0.021]$, $0.012$ $[0.001, 0.022]$ below $k = 12$ and $0.013$ $[0.005, 0.019]$ below $k = 8$ (on seeds 12--16, $+0.010$ $[-0.003, +0.023]$ and $0.007$ $[-0.004, +0.017]$ below $k = 12$), and its price is $0.017$ $[0.006, 0.031]$, against $0.022$ for $k = 12$ and $0.015$ for $k = 8$. Under error from the first rating it gains $+0.011$ $[-0.005, +0.026]$, $0.007$ $[-0.003, +0.017]$ more than $k = 12$ and level with $k = 8$ ($-0.003$ $[-0.010, +0.004]$). From trial 21 it gains $+0.007$ $[-0.003, +0.017]$, $0.031$ $[0.016, 0.044]$ below $k = 12$, most of it in the $5\sigma$ cell, where it gains $+0.036$ and $k = 12$ gains $+0.163$; without that cell it is $0.005$ $[-0.004, +0.013]$ above $k = 12$ over both onsets. Its search term credits every trial kept for searching with the recent gain in best rating, and rating error inflates that gain. Over the ten trials before each decision point after trial 21, the gain per trial in best rating is $6$ to $15$ times the gain in best true value among the same designs at $1\sigma$ and $31$ to $135$ times at $5\sigma$ (ratios of landscape means; above $3$ and $13$ under each of the four processes), against at most $2.8$ and $13$ times under error from the first rating. At $5\sigma$ from trial 21 it buys a sitting in $73\%$ of runs, of median size five, where a sitting of $16$ gains $+0.227$ $[+0.147, +0.321]$ on seeds 12--16 (\autoref{tab:sittingcell}). Given the gain in best true value instead, which no experimenter has, its gain from trial 21 rises to $+0.019$ $[+0.005, +0.033]$ and its shortfall to $k = 12$ falls to $0.019$ $[0.007, 0.030]$: the inflated search term accounts for about two fifths of that shortfall, and the rest is not explained here. At half the idiosyncratic SD the decide-once rule gains $+0.004$ $[-0.013, +0.021]$ against $+0.051$ $[+0.028, +0.072]$ for $k = 16$, and the sequential form $+0.033$ $[+0.020, +0.046]$, $0.018$ $[0.006, 0.030]$ below $k = 16$.

\subsection{Budget-neutral arms}
\label{app:budgetneutral}

Every arm below keeps the number of human trials equal to the standard process or lowers it, and is scored against the standard process by the estimand of Appendix~\ref{app:adapt}: twenty landscapes, seeds 7--11, both onsets where the arm has them. The halo arm is the exception: it runs four multi-objective problems (BraninCurrin, ZDT1, DTLZ2 and VehicleSafety) with qLogNEHVI at $1\sigma$ on seeds 7--16, and divides each problem's regret by its hypervolume floor gap (Section~\ref{sec:multiobjective}), since a Pareto problem has no achievable improvement. \autoref{tab:budgetneutral} gives the share of the standard process's cost of error that each recovers on the design the study deploys, and the price each pays without error.

The faults in the table are defined as follows. A \emph{spike} adds, with probability $0.05$ or $0.15$ per trial, an independent draw of $5$ or $20$ landscape SDs on top of the rating error. The clipping remedy truncates every noisy rating to the landscape's range, from the minimum of its $65{,}536$-point Sobol sample to its known optimum, the analogue of a bounded rating scale whose top is the best design. A study would have to know that range, which is known here by construction. The clean twin is never clipped, so the remedy's zero price is structural rather than measured. The relevance-pursuit remedy \citep{ament2024robust} lets a sparse set of trials carry their own inflated noise variance. A \emph{relay} of raters gives each rater a fixed offset, drawn with twice the SD of the rating error, and hands over either every ten trials (block) or trial by trial among five raters (round robin). The remedy estimates the offsets inside the GP fit. A \emph{rating cap} truncates every noisy rating at the landscape's $0.9$ quantile, so the best designs become indistinguishable. The re-anchored remedy lifts the cap to half a landscape SD above the true value of the best design rated so far. \emph{Halo} error, in the multi-objective arm, makes one trial's rating errors share a common factor across objectives, at correlation $0.85$. The backfit estimates that factor from the residuals and refits. A \emph{lost rating} never arrives, either at random or only for designs below the landscape's $40$th percentile. The standard process drops the trial, and the arm instead imputes the $20$th percentile of the ratings so far and never deploys the imputed design.

Changes to the search fail. What helps is a change to how the ratings are read or produced, though the clipping remedy's range ends at the known optimum. Clipping the response to the landscape's known range recovers $6$--$35\%$ of the cost of gross faults, or $24$--$44\%$ when its tied top ratings are broken at random, at a price that is zero by construction; a relevance-pursuit likelihood recovers $26$--$38\%$ when $15\%$ of trials carry a gross fault and recovers nothing measurable when only $5\%$ do, and without error it costs $1.1$ points of the achievable improvement on the deployed design ($1.2$ on the trajectory); fitting per-rater offsets recovers $32\%$ $[23, 40]$ when raters alternate, at a price of $0.7$ points without error, and nothing when they hand over in blocks, because a block offset is confounded with the search's own time trend while an interleaved one is identifiable; re-anchoring a saturating cap recovers $25\%$ $[17, 32]$ at a price that is zero by construction, since, like the clipping, the cap never acts on the clean twin ($69\%$ $[62, 76]$ at $0.25\sigma$, $-7\%$ $[-15, +1]$ at $1\sigma$), or $18\%$ $[9, 25]$ when tied ratings at the cap are broken at random ($64\%$ $[56, 71]$ and $-17\%$ $[-25, -10]$), so under that convention it loses at $1\sigma$; and removing the factor shared across objectives recovers $24\%$ of the deployed loss of halo error in units of each problem's floor gap, positive on all four problems ($+1\%$ on DTLZ2, $+23\%$ on VehicleSafety, $+37\%$ on BraninCurrin and $+51\%$ on ZDT1) at a price of $-0.3$ points, though four problems allow no test (the smallest two-sided Wilcoxon $p$ they can give is $0.125$), and it does not help the trajectory ($-3\%$, the problems ranging from $-30\%$ to $+21\%$), so it is not a demonstrated remedy. A saturating rating scale is itself among the most expensive faults we measured, costing $185\%$ $[132, 258]$ more than the same rating noise on a scale that does not saturate, and $135\%$ $[92, 196]$ more when tied ratings at the cap are broken at random rather than in favour of the earliest design to reach it.

\subsection{Process adaptations}
\label{app:adapt}

The measurements above say what error costs a fixed process. The practical question is whether the process can be changed to cost less, and the scoring has to change with it. Rating a design twice, or re-rating designs before deploying, also changes the clean run, because a repeated rating of an exact objective carries no information, so a process change scored by its excess over its own clean run is measured against a handicapped reference. We score each change against the \emph{standard} process instead. Per paired cell (landscape, acquisition, magnitude, onset, seed), with regret as a fraction of the achievable improvement,
\begin{equation*}
  \text{recovered} = \frac{R^{\text{std}}_{\text{noisy}} - R^{\text{adapt}}_{\text{noisy}}}
                          {R^{\text{std}}_{\text{noisy}} - R^{\text{std}}_{\text{clean}}},
  \qquad
  \text{price} = R^{\text{adapt}}_{\text{clean}} - R^{\text{std}}_{\text{clean}},
\end{equation*}
aggregated as ratios of landscape means with landscape-bootstrap intervals, on two responses: the post-onset trajectory regret and the regret of the design the experimenter would deploy. qKG, re-rating the incumbent, augmented EI and Thompson sampling replace the acquisition, so there the standard process is LogEI, the suite's default. Every arm runs twenty landscapes, seeds 7--11, both onsets and the error process named in \autoref{tab:adapt}, with LogEI and qNEI unless stated. The fitted-oracle row runs the three archival datasets on seeds 7--16 and divides each dataset's regret by its achievable improvement, the mean over seeds of the fitted oracle's optimum less its mean over the search box; three datasets allow no test, and its bracket is the range of the three per-dataset values. The incumbent and known-noise rows re-score the arms of Appendix~\ref{sec:noisediag} and~\ref{sec:incumbent} with their own acquisitions.

Scoring an arm that replaces the acquisition against the mean of the ten standard acquisitions is not like for like: that reference carries the four weakest acquisitions' cost, and augmented EI then ``recovers'' $+373\%$ on the trajectory at $0.05\sigma$ from trial 21, which would mean the arm under error beats the standard process with no error at all. The ten-mean figures are kept beside the LogEI ones in the analysis output. Shares of a cost are ratios of landscape means in units of the achievable improvement. Formed from raw landscape-SD values instead, the landscapes with the largest $\optz$ dominate, and the incumbent arm's own-twin share of Appendix~\ref{sec:incumbent} reads $15.6\%$ rather than $20.0\%$.

\textbf{Two of the paper's own ablations, re-scored.} The observed-max incumbent and the known-noise GP change the clean run too, so their shares in Appendix~\ref{sec:noisediag} and~\ref{sec:incumbent} are also excess over their own clean runs. Against the standard process the conclusions stand, with one addition. The incumbent recovers $19\%$ $[10, 27]$ of the trajectory loss at a price of $0.1$ points without error, most of it from the first rating ($+47\%$, $+24\%$ and $+10\%$ at $0.25\sigma$, $1\sigma$ and $5\sigma$; at $0.05\sigma$ the reference cost, $0.010$, is negative on one landscape, so only the gain is given, $0.007$ of the achievable improvement), and cuts the extra trials needed, over that arm's seven acquisitions, to match a clean standard ten-trial study at $1\sigma$ from $8$ ($21\%$ never) to $6$ ($13\%$), and at $5\sigma$ from $36$ ($48\%$) to $21$ ($39\%$). The design it deploys is worse, though, by $10\%$ of the cost $[5, 15]$, mostly at the late onset, for a reason we have not isolated. Giving the GP the true noise variance recovers nothing on either response: $-3\%$ $[-16, +8]$ of the trajectory loss and $-3\%$ $[-11, +5]$ of the deployed design's, at a price without error of $0.4$ points on the trajectory and none on the deployed design. The $+3\%$ of Appendix~\ref{sec:noisediag} is the same arm measured against its own clean run, and no cell survives correction there either.

\textbf{Rating early designs twice makes things worse.} The onset effect says the first trials are where error is expensive, so replicating them is the natural fix. It is not one: rating each of the first ten proposals twice recovers $-88\%$ of the trajectory loss and $-14\%$ of the deployed design's (\autoref{tab:adapt}), and it needs more extra trials than the standard process on the same arm to match a clean standard ten-trial study, a median of $8$ at $1\sigma$ against $6$. In a fifty-trial study the ten designs it gives up cost more than averaging two ratings, which only divides the error variance by two, buys. The GP already pools neighbouring observations. Adding the observed-max incumbent of Appendix~\ref{sec:incumbent} does not rescue it ($-44\%$ on the deployed design), and on the three fitted-oracle datasets (seeds 7--16) the same replication recovers $-5\%$ of the deployed design's cost (\texttt{ehmi} $+6\%$, \texttt{opticarvis} $-21\%$, \texttt{provoice} $+1\%$) and costs the trajectory on all three ($-67\%$; $-49\%$, $-78\%$ and $-78\%$ in the same order), at a price without error of $1.1$ points of the achievable improvement on the deployed design and $2.8$ on the trajectory; three datasets allow no test. Re-rating the incumbent every second trial fails for the same reason ($-114\%$ $[-155, -76]$ on the trajectory). qKG helps only at $5\sigma$ from the first rating ($+21\%$ on the trajectory) and does not help elsewhere, for a reason we have not tested (Appendix~\ref{sec:designchoices}).

\textbf{Re-evaluating before deploying helps, a little.} Spending the last six trials re-rating the three designs with the best mean rating, and deploying by mean, costs almost nothing in the trajectory ($-1\%$) and recovers $4\%$ of the deployed design's cost, $[0.04, 9]$, reaching $18\%$ $[5, 29]$ when large error arrives late. Under an unnoticed slip it recovers nothing ($-6\%$, $[-15, 1]$): the re-ratings land on displaced points too, so averaging them does not correct the record.

\textbf{A noisy-input GP helps small early slips and hurts large late ones.} Inflating each point's observation variance by the squared gradient of the posterior mean times the slip variance \citep{mchutchon2011gaussian} gains $0.012$ and $0.010$ of the achievable improvement on the trajectory under $1\%$ and $5\%$ slips from the first trial, whose costs ($0.016$ and $0.061$) are negative on seven and one of the twenty landscapes, so no share is given, at a price without error of $-0.1$ points; at the late onset it recovers $-129\%$ and $-205\%$ of the cost of $15\%$ and $40\%$ slips. A likely reason, which we have not tested, is that by then the search sits near a steep peak whose flanks have the largest gradients, so the observations that locate the peak are discounted most. Pooled it recovers $-28\%$ of the trajectory loss.

\textbf{A heavy-tailed likelihood does not absorb misclicks.} A misclick records the rating of a random design against the proposed one, an outlier rather than noise, so a Student-$t$ likelihood should discount it. A variational Student-$t$ GP \citep{jylanki2011robust, martinezcantin2018practical} in place of the standard one recovers $-2\%$ $[-38, 31]$ of the trajectory loss and $-13\%$ $[-50, 16]$ of the deployed design's, at a price of $0.2$ and $1.5$ points without error. That GP also swaps the RBF kernel for a Mat\'ern-5/2 and marginal likelihood for a variational bound. Rerun with the standard RBF kernel, so that only the likelihood and its variational fit differ, it does worse: $-58\%$ $[-123, -5]$ and $-60\%$ $[-114, -10]$, at a price of $3.4$ and $5.5$ points without error (\autoref{tab:adapt}). The kernel change did not mask a benefit of the likelihood. Where misclicks cost most, on $40\%$ of trials from the first, the Mat\'ern version recovers $-1\%$ $[-10, 6]$ of the trajectory loss and the RBF version $+5\%$ $[-8, 16]$, and the median run of either still needs $8$ extra trials to match a clean standard ten-trial study, as the standard process does on this arm ($24\%$ and $21\%$ never, against $28\%$). Misclicks cost little in the first place, so there is little for any model to recover.

Every remedy in Appendix~\ref{app:budgetneutral} changes the loop. These four change what the study deploys, how it reads the ratings it already has, or what it asks the rater, and three of them are scored by re-reading runs that already exist (\texttt{scripts/replay\_hitl\_remedies.py}), so they cost nothing to evaluate as well as nothing to run. The estimand is the one of Appendix~\ref{app:adapt}: a share of the standard process's cost of error on the design the study deploys, as a ratio of landscape means with a landscape bootstrap.

\paragraph{A shortlist instead of a design.} \autoref{tab:shortlist} gives the curve. The gain is entirely the width of the deliverable: at $m=1$ the same rule recovers nothing on the main sweep. And the score is the regret of the \emph{best} of the $m$, which a later decision would have to find. It is therefore an upper bound on what shipping a shortlist delivers, to be read as the size of the prize for any downstream discrimination that is cheaper than a human rating. Where no such decision exists the number is unavailable, and a study should say so.

\paragraph{Choosing on ranks.} Refitting the surrogate on the ratings' normal scores instead of their values makes the choice invariant to any strictly monotone distortion of the scale and robust to a single wild rating. On the main sweep, where discarding the magnitudes throws information away, it recovers $-1\%$ $[-11, +8]$ of the cost of error. Its price without error is $0.027$ of the achievable improvement over seeds 7--16 ($0.029$ on seeds 7--11 and $0.024$ on seeds 12--16), against $0.022$ for the cautious rule on the same acquisitions, LogEI and qNEI. It recovers $51\%$ $[36, 63]$ under gross faults and $36\%$ $[29, 43]$ against a saturating cap on seeds 7--11, and $49\%$ $[34, 63]$ and $38\%$ $[31, 45]$ on seeds 12--16, which were run for this check after the rule was chosen (Appendix~\ref{app:heldout}). Against the cap, part of that recovery is the standard rule's tie-break: with tied ratings at the cap broken at random, the rank refit recovers $23\%$ $[17, 28]$ on seeds 7--11 and $21\%$ $[16, 27]$ on seeds 12--16. Under gross faults on $15\%$ of trials it does at least as well as the relevance-pursuit likelihood ($38\%$ $[24, 51]$) of Appendix~\ref{app:budgetneutral} while assuming nothing about the fault. The contrast is the finding: a rank-based reading is not a general improvement. It is an outlier remedy that costs no rating trial but is not free. Without error it pays its price, and under the main sweep's rating noise, which has no gross faults, it recovers nothing, so, like the cautious ship rule, it should not be used without a reason to expect the fault.

\paragraph{Asking the rater how sure they were.} A self-reported precision, used as a per-trial observation variance, recovers $15\%$ $[6, 23]$ of the deployed loss on seeds 7--11 at a price of $-0.001$ (\autoref{tab:adapt}), and $11\%$ $[-2, 23]$ on seeds 12--16, run for this check after the arm was built. It is worth setting beside the known-noise arm, which is handed the \emph{true} noise variance and recovers nothing: a constant is what the GP already learns, and a per-trial report says which trials were hard. How much it says is the whole question. The first model took the report to be the trial's squared error seen through a log-normal multiplier of SD $0.5$, which gives the rater a rank correlation of about $0.96$ with their own realised error, close to clairvoyance. Three further arms name that correlation instead, through a Gaussian copula that keeps the error's marginal, and run in the two cells where the first model's recovery is well above zero ($0.25\sigma$ and $1\sigma$ from the first rating, seeds 7--11). Pooled over those cells the first model recovers $31\%$ of the deployed loss; at a correlation of $0.6$ the report recovers $12\%$ $[-1, 23]$, at $0.3$ it recovers $-10\%$ $[-25, +4]$, and an uninformative report, at $0$, $-16\%$ $[-37, +2]$, presumably because the GP then trusts per-trial variances that are wrong. A study should ask for confidence only if it knows its raters' confidence tracks their errors.

\paragraph{Screening the instrument.} A capped scale costs the deployed design $185\%$ more than the same noise on a scale that does not saturate ($135\%$ with tied ratings at the cap broken at random), and how often the cap binds is fixed by its quantile. What varies, and what a pilot can measure, is the ceiling gap, how far the reachable optimum sits above the ceiling. Across the $40$ landscape-by-magnitude cells ($0.25\sigma$ and $1\sigma$) that gap predicts the extra cost at Spearman $0.65$, and the extra cost doubles from $0.153$ in the half of the landscapes with the smaller gap to $0.316$ in the half with the larger gap. Re-anchoring the cap to the best design rated so far halves the median damage, from $0.219$ to $0.104$. These values compare the capped arm's LogEI and qNEI runs on seeds 7--11 with the main sweep's mean over its ten acquisitions and ten seeds, on the deployed design at $0.25\sigma$ and $1\sigma$ from the first rating; against the same main-sweep runs (LogEI and qNEI, seeds 7--11) the gap predicts the extra cost at Spearman $0.49$, the halves are $0.200$ and $0.363$, and re-anchoring takes the median damage from $0.255$ to $0.131$ ($0.64$, $0.125$ and $0.286$, and $0.176$ to $0.114$, with ties broken at random). With tied ratings at the cap broken at random, the gap predicts the extra cost at Spearman $0.76$, the extra cost is $0.079$ and $0.238$ in the two halves, and re-anchoring cuts the median damage by about a third, from $0.132$ to $0.084$. An instrument can therefore be rejected before a participant is spent on it.

\paragraph{What did not work.} Six further ideas were built and measured, and they show where the cost lives. Allocating the final sitting's looks where the ranking is in doubt, by the standard best-arm rule \citep{audibert2010best, kim2006selecting}, is never ahead of one look each ($+9.0\%$ against $+9.7\%$ at $k=8$ and the full idiosyncratic SD): the gain is having looks, not where they go. Sizing a study from the pilot's $\frag$ does no better than sizing it at random, although any uneven allocation of a fixed budget beats an even one. Interleaving fixed anchor designs to identify the rater's drift does not help under drift ($-6\%$ $[-15, +1]$; $+1\%$ under \textsc{gaussian}, \autoref{tab:adapt}) and costs trials. Rating the first five proposals late instead of early moves nothing ($+1\%$), which rules out one explanation of the onset effect. Judging each proposal beside the incumbent, so the error shared by the pair cancels, \emph{loses} $27\%$: it pays $\sqrt2$ more fresh noise to remove faults the loop already absorbs, since it standardises its targets and an argmax is shift-invariant. An acquisition aimed at the ship rule rather than the posterior maximum loses $107\%$, but its price without error is $+0.244$, the largest in this paper, so it searches badly and its own idea is untested.

\subsection{Comparisons instead of ratings: what the invariance does and does not buy}
\label{app:elicitation}

Preference elicitation is the usual response to an unreliable rater \citep{chu2005preference, gonzalez2017preferential}, on the argument that a comparison is invariant to how the rater uses the scale. The argument is exact and its reach is narrow, and both halves are measurable here. A fault is \emph{shared} when it acts on every design of one sitting through the same monotone map $\psi$; then for strictly increasing $\psi$, $\psi(g(a)) > \psi(g(b)) \Leftrightarrow g(a) > g(b)$, and the comparison is exactly what it would have been with no fault. A fault drawn afresh for each judgement cancels nothing, and a comparison inherits the noise of both designs.

We ran two loops at equal human cost on the twenty landscapes, three seeds (7--9) and $T=20$ judgements (\texttt{scripts/elicitation\_compare.py}): the standard rating loop, which ships the earliest design with the best rating, and a comparison loop with a Laplace-approximate \textsc{PairwiseGP} \citep{balandat2020botorch} on which each trial proposes one design and asks for a comparison with the incumbent, shipping the posterior-mean maximiser. Every run seeds all its random streams, the library's included, from its own seed. Of the comparison loop's $7{,}680$ fits $20$ fell back to prior hyperparameters, and none of the rating loop's $7{,}200$; they are counted, not dropped. Each loop is scored against its own identically seeded clean twin and, because the comparison loop also changes the clean run, against the standard process as well (Appendix~\ref{app:adapt}), with the rating loop as the reference and the comparison loop's clean run paying a price. Every number below is the deployed design at the final judgement, a landscape mean in units of the achievable improvement with a landscape-bootstrap interval.

The invariance under a shared strictly monotone fault is not a finding but a consequence of the construction above, and the run checks the implementation rather than the claim: under \textsc{drift} the rating loop's shipped design differs from its clean twin in $48$ of $60$ runs, at an excess of $0.019$ of the achievable improvement, and the comparison loop's in \emph{none}. What the run does test is the reach of the invariance, and there it is narrow. Under a saturating scale, a map that is monotone but not strictly so, two designs above the cap are indistinguishable, and near the optimum that is exactly the pair the study must tell apart. There the comparison loop's excess over its own twin is $0.148$ against the rating loop's $0.170$, so it removes $13\%$ $[-18, +37]$ of the rating loop's own-twin cost, and $-3\%$ $[-35, +23]$ when the ratings tied at the cap, which decide the shipped design in $56$ of the rating loop's $60$ runs, are broken at random. Against the standard process it recovers $-6\%$ $[-37, +18]$ of the rating loop's cost of the cap, or $-26\%$ $[-54, -3]$ with the ties broken at random, at a price of $0.033$ without error. Against the standard process it gains $-0.014$ of the achievable improvement under the bare \textsc{drift}, whose cost to the rating loop ($0.019$) is negative on five of the twenty landscapes, so no share is given, and $-0.033$, its whole price, under the bare \textsc{offset}, which costs the rating loop nothing. Under \textsc{gaussian} noise, drawn afresh per judgement, it recovers $-32\%$ $[-77, -1]$ of the cost against the standard process ($-19\%$ $[-65, +10]$ against its own twin), at the same price. The direction is the one the $\sqrt2$ argument predicts, and the magnitude is not established. The three shared faults of this arm carry \emph{no} idiosyncratic noise: the \textsc{drift} here is a bare ramp, the \textsc{offset} a bare constant and the cap a bare truncation, whereas the sweep's processes of the same name add a \textsc{gaussian} draw on top. A noise-free strictly monotone fault is the case a comparison is immune to, so the $48$-against-none result above states the construction rather than measuring a remedy; with the sweep's noise added the comparison would inherit $\sqrt2$ of it. Read this arm as an upper bound. Under a constant \textsc{offset} present from the first trial, with no noise added, neither loop's design moves in any run, also by construction: a rating loop that standardises its outputs and ships an argmax is already shift-equivariant, so that arm tests the rating loop's invariance rather than the comparison's, and the cost of \textsc{bias} elsewhere in this paper comes from its noise and from its arriving \emph{part-way through}.

So changing the elicitation is not a general remedy. It removes, exactly, the effect on its own run of that part of the fault which is a strictly monotone reparametrisation of the scale (drift, a rater's personal offset, a handover between raters), but in this arm its price without error exceeds what that removes, and it removes nothing of the inconsistency, the saturation or the gross faults.

\subsection{Detecting a mid-study change, and whether the detector matters}
\label{app:detector}

A rater who becomes noisier part-way through a study leaves a trace in the loop's own surrogate. With its hyperparameters and standardisation frozen on the first $n_0 = 15$ trials, the one-step-ahead standardised residual $z_t$ is standard normal while the rater behaves and its variance inflates when the rater does not. The freeze rule runs a one-sided cumulative-sum (CUSUM) test \citep{page1954continuous} on $z_t^2$, alarms at the first $t$ with $S_t > h$, and on alarm stops the study and ships the visited design with the highest posterior mean less one latent SD, refitted on the trials before the alarm window. Its thresholds are tuned on seeds 7--11 to a false-alarm budget of at most $10\%$ on runs in which nothing changes after $n_0$, and scored on seeds 12--16. Pooled over processes, magnitudes and onsets it recovers $14.7\%$ $[3, 26]$ of the deployed loss at a price of $0.028$ of the achievable improvement on the deployed design without error ($0.007$ of it from stopping and the rest from the change of ship rule), while saving $4.5$ of $50$ trials. It raises a false alarm on $6.6\%$ of the held-out runs with error from the first rating, in which nothing changes after $n_0$, and on $7.5\%$ of the clean runs; on the held-out seeds no run whose error arrives at trial 21 alarms before trial 21, so over all held-out runs with error the false-alarm rate is $3.3\%$. The effect sits where it should: for a $5\sigma$ error arriving at trial 21 it recovers $42\%$ $[25, 59]$ (\textsc{bias}) to $64\%$ $[51, 75]$ (\textsc{drift}), detects $94$--$98\%$ of onsets with a median delay of $3$--$5$ trials and saves $21$--$24$ trials; for a $1\sigma$ error arriving at trial 21 detection is $48$--$58\%$ and recovery $-9\%$ to $+7\%$, indistinguishable from zero; for an error present from the first rating there is nothing to detect.

A CUSUM is a choice, not a derivation, so we compare it with two alternatives on exactly the same $13{,}200$ residual streams ($6{,}600$ tuning, $6{,}600$ held out; $6{,}400$ carrying a real mid-study change). Since the residual is meant to be standard normal while the rater behaves, and a rater who becomes noisier inflates its variance, every detector tests $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ against $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$, $\sigma>1$: the CUSUM; the generalised likelihood ratio (GLR) maximised over the unknown change point \citep{lorden1971procedures}, whose statistic $\max_\tau \tfrac{n}{2}(\hat\sigma^2_\tau - 1 - \log\hat\sigma^2_\tau)$ is the classical answer to that question and what a CUSUM approximates; and Bayesian online change-point detection \citep{adams2007bayesian} with a constant hazard and a normal--inverse-gamma model of the post-change variance, alarming when the posterior probability that the current run is short exceeds a threshold. Each detector's threshold is tuned on the tuning seeds to a common false-alarm budget (an alarm is false when the rater never changed), and scored on the held-out seeds. Two caveats bound what the comparison can show. The standardised residual is not standard normal while the rater behaves: its root mean square runs from $0.59$ to $11.4$ across landscapes, so the tuned thresholds are large and the CUSUM and the GLR are both dominated by the same largest squared residual, firing together on all but one of the held-out runs. The budget is also common only in the pool: at a nominal $5\%$ the held-out false-alarm rate is $0\%$ on Branin and $29\%$ on Shekel.

\textbf{The detector is not the bottleneck.} At a $10\%$ budget the GLR detects $45.5\%$ of changes and the CUSUM $45.4\%$; at $5\%$, $36.8\%$ against $36.9\%$; at $20\%$, $56.0\%$ against $56.4\%$. The CUSUM matches the classical optimum for this alternative to within half a percentage point at every budget. That CUSUM is re-tuned for the comparison, its drift $\kappa$ chosen from $0.25$ to $2$ on the tuning seeds ($0.25$ at every budget) with no window. The freeze rule's own detector has $\kappa = 16$ and $h = 2000$, and its window $w = 3$ only sets the prefix on which the shipped design is refitted. At that operating point, a false-alarm rate of $6.9\%$ on the tuning seeds and $6.6\%$ held out, it detects $41.5\%$ of the held-out changes with a median delay of $8$ trials; the GLR tuned to the same rate detects $41.4\%$ and Bayesian online detection $27.0\%$. With $\kappa = 16$ at the three common budgets the CUSUM detects $36.8\%$, $45.4\%$ and $54.1\%$, against the GLR's $36.8\%$, $45.5\%$ and $56.0\%$. There is nothing to be gained by replacing the freeze rule's detector at its operating point. Bayesian online detection is worse on detection rate at every budget ($23.0\%$, $32.2\%$ and $45.1\%$ at $5$, $10$ and $20\%$) but alarms with a median delay of $2$ trials against the CUSUM's $6$--$9$ pooled over magnitudes. Whether that trade pays depends on how fast the freeze rule's gain decays with delay, which we have not measured. It is a choice of operating point rather than a better method. What limits the freeze rule is the signal, not the statistic computed from it: a rater who becomes somewhat noisier midway leaves little of one.

The headroom control exists to stop a landscape with nothing to lose from contributing a null that looks like evidence of robustness. That is a reason to read the sensitivity analysis as informative. It is not a reason to call the run confirmed, and we do not.
